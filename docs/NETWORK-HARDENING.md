# Network resource controls

Hashstash bounds local resources used by untrusted peer, RPC, and mining traffic. These are node policies: exceeding a traffic budget does not make a block invalid.

## Isolation and admission

RPC requires a loopback listener, including on nodes without an enabled wallet. RPC, peer traffic, and Stratum use separate session and worker capacity. Per-IP counters are scoped to each service; a peer cooldown cannot also disable wallet access from that IP. IPv4-mapped IPv6 addresses share the IPv4 counter, a 6to4 source shares one counter with the /48 its IPv4 host controls, and another global-unicast IPv6 source shares one counter with its whole /64, because one host normally controls that prefix. Connections this node opens use records separate from inbound traffic from the same address, so inbound abuse or a cooldown caused by another host behind a shared address cannot block this node's own synchronization with it.

Admission checks run before large reads and expensive work. Request, byte, and work budgets refill over time. Accounting is bounded to 1,024 address/service/direction entries, and active entries cannot be evicted; when the table is full, an inactive entry whose cooldown has ended is replaced before one still cooling down. Temporary cooldowns increase from 250 milliseconds up to 16 seconds. They apply to malformed frames or payloads and explicit resource violations; ordinary stale transactions and competing branches are not grounds for a permanent ban. An HTTP request that admission refuses is answered, never dropped. An address cooldown or an exhausted connection, request, byte or work budget returns 429 with `Retry-After`: the whole seconds until the cooldown has ended and the refused charge has refilled. Busy sessions, RPC workers or parsers return 503 with `Retry-After: 1`. JSON-RPC requests keep their JSON-RPC error body and ID. A refused request has not run, so a client may resend it after that wait; a `mine` batch refused after its first block instead reports how many blocks it mined, as an ordinary error. A charge larger than the whole burst can never be admitted and returns 400. A connection refused before its session starts is answered once its request head arrives, within two seconds and the 16 KiB head limit, and its body is discarded within the usual bound; at most 16 such answers per listener are in flight, and past that a refused connection is closed unanswered. Each answer is a short fixed-shape error, so refusing stays cheaper than the request it refuses. A failed authenticated handshake closes without a cooldown, because nothing has been proved and the source address may be shared; connection-rate limits still apply. Exhausting the budget for a reply this node chose to send fails that reply without penalizing the recipient. Configured-peer retries back off with jitter rather than reconnecting at a fixed rate after every failure. Stratum requires authorization within 10 seconds; an expired attempt closes the connection, and a failed password also starts a cooldown for that address. Connections awaiting authorization have their own slots and leave them once authorized, so unauthenticated sockets cannot occupy authorized worker slots. Stratum admission, with its request unit and cooldown check, runs before a waiting slot is chosen, so a refused attempt still spends its address's budget. Waiting is charged to the source address however the connection ends: each attempt reserves a whole 10-second window from a budget of four windows that refills by half a second per second, and the unused part returns at authorization or close. One address therefore waits in at most four slots at once, and closing sockets just before the window ends leaves it about half a slot on average. At most eight connections from addresses that have not recently authorized may wait at once from one group (IPv4 /24, IPv6 /48; a source on a local network is its own group). The 256 addresses that most recently authorized are not charged, are exempt from the group limit and may use 32 reserved slots when the 128 general slots are full, so the operator's miners can reconnect. Filling the general slots takes at least 16 public groups and keeping them full takes far more addresses. After a restart no address has authorized yet, and none of this protects against a distributed flood. A non-loopback Stratum listener requires a password. Authorized solo miners can remain connected while waiting to find a block. An address cooldown refuses new Stratum connections and unauthenticated requests but does not end an authorized worker's requests, because another device behind a shared address may have caused it; the address budgets still apply.

Inbound peer sessions use 16 general slots. At most two concurrent sessions may come from one network group: an IPv4 /16, an IPv6 /32, or for a 6to4 source the IPv4 /16 it embeds. Only loopback, link-local, unique-local, private and shared (CGNAT) sources keep per-address limits alone; routable special ranges such as Teredo are grouped by /32. When the general slots are full, configured and pinned peer addresses can use four reserved slots that no other source can take, without a group cap. Discovery peers this node synchronized with during the last hour have four separate reserved slots, at most one per network group, and their group cap still applies, because any remote can arrange to be verified. Each inbound session has an absolute deadline of twice the peer read allowance. That deadline also covers waits for local workers and validation, so its expiry closes the session without a cooldown; a peer that misses an individual read allowance is penalized. The handshake and first message share one read allowance. An outbound synchronization, including its wait for one of the two outbound slots, has one overall deadline of five read allowances. Accept errors never stop a listener or abort its live sessions: a reset, aborted or refused connection is one client's queued connection and is skipped at once however often it repeats, an interrupted call is retried at once up to 64 consecutive times, other errors back off from 10 milliseconds to one second, and counts and the last error appear in `peerstatus.listeners`. Stratum extranonces are allocated only for mining sessions and skip values held by connected sessions, so no listener depends on a lifetime connection count. Only peer synchronization failures enter the `peers` diagnostic ring; web and mining clients cannot push them out.

The table gives baseline byte bursts. The private node's RPC and peer byte bursts grow with the highest validated block capacity. They cover the greater of the 66 MiB baseline, the peer frame allowance, or twice that capacity plus 128 KiB for a hexadecimal JSON envelope and 16 KiB for HTTP headers, within the host's allocation representation. A published capacity increase refreshes the allowance immediately; refill rates and other quotas do not change. Public-web uses a separate guard. Its byte burst is the HTTP response limit described below, at least 193 MiB, so one maximal valid reply always fits once the budget refills. Connection admission also consumes a request unit. Each Stratum session has a further limit of 8 requests per second with a burst of 16. An unauthenticated session that exceeds it is closed without penalizing its address. An authorized worker's lines past it draw on a separate allowance of 8 per second with a burst of 64 and spend no address request units, so one fast worker cannot drain the budget of others behind its address; such a line can still submit a block, while any other request or share it carries is refused with a rate-limit error. A worker that exhausts that allowance too is closed without penalty. Peer byte and work budgets cover replies as well as incoming traffic, preventing small locator requests from generating unlimited large responses. A message costs one work unit per loose transaction and per block plus its transactions; a receiver also charges its current pool size, selection plus backlog, when loose transactions arrive. One reply's block work is limited to 16,384 minus 256 loose transactions, the 4,096-entry selection and the 256-entry backlog, so a complete reply fits one second of peer work refill; the first block is always sent. The peer work burst grows, if needed, to admit one block at the genesis signature limit with a full loose batch and pool. Inbound peer frame memory is reserved as bytes arrive, never for a declared length, so buffered memory always costs the sender those bytes of its byte budget. Across all sessions it is limited to the greater of 64 MiB and twice the largest validated frame allowance. Sessions other than configured or pinned peers share all of it except one largest frame, and one network group may hold the greater of one largest frame and an eighth of that share, so configured and pinned peers can always receive a full frame. A frame beyond its budget closes without a cooldown. Work units are operation-cost estimates, not hashes or wall-clock CPU measurements. There are two parser workers per service, four RPC read workers, two RPC mutation workers, two peer workers, and two mining workers. Worker permits remain held until the work finishes even if a connection closes.

The public-web listener has its own pool of 32 sessions and its own parser, read and mutation workers. A reverse proxy on loopback carries every visitor, so its record has the RPC request, byte and work budgets above, may hold all 32 sessions and is never cooled down. When that proxy sends `X-Real-IP`, the node also accounts the named visitor in a separate record, which the proxy must make trustworthy by overwriting the header; it is ignored from any other address. Each named visitor, and each client connecting directly from another address, may hold 6 sessions and spend an eighth of the work budget (16,384 units per second, burst 131,072). A named visitor pays from its own record before the shared one, so it cannot spend more than that share of what all visitors share. The request line and headers must arrive within 5 seconds. HTTP framing errors and expired deadlines cool down a remote client or named visitor, but never local software on loopback; refused origins and unknown paths, which ordinary browsers cause, are not penalized.

The configured Stratum reporting difficulty defaults to 65,536. Choose it for the expected hash rate and traffic budget. When a connection's valid reporting shares at its current difficulty arrive faster than two per second, measured over at least four share intervals and at least 250 milliseconds, its difficulty for new jobs is multiplied by the power of two that brings the observed rate to about one per second. A stream far above the session rate is therefore raised in one step, and the shares it sends past that rate before it switches jobs are refused rather than disconnected. The per-IP request budget still bounds the combined share rate of all workers behind one address. Each job's reporting difficulty is capped at network difficulty so every block solution is reported. BIP310 minimum-difficulty negotiation must remain compatible with the fixed genesis target limit, including when timestamp ASERT makes mining easier. An extremely easy chain therefore needs an appropriate starting target before ASIC-scale mining. Valid non-block shares are acknowledged without executing the block. Each job caches its coinbase fragments and retains its assigned reporting target. A connection keeps its eight most recent jobs; a parent change discards previous jobs, while a same-parent refresh retires only the oldest. Same-parent template refreshes reach a connection at most once every two seconds. A solved block waits for a parser and a mining worker without a deadline. An authorized worker's line is handled before its charges are enforced, so neither a budget or rate limit at its address, nor an address cooldown, nor the block's work charge can discard a solution. Duplicate header tracking spans job IDs on the same parent. Worker records are removed on disconnect, and hashrate samples use a bounded rolling window. Reporting shares never create issuance or alter fork choice. See [mining setup](MINING.md).

Wallet status, account, governance, quote and transaction-preparation reads use one immutable published view. They can return the latest completed state while another worker validates incoming history. State-changing requests and historical block reads can still wait for chain access. The view is published after accepted changes, including accepted blocks preceding a later rejected block in the same peer exchange. Tasks on the asynchronous runtime never wait for chain access: peer frame limits and exchange deadlines use the published configuration and the greatest validated capacity, Stratum stale-job checks compare with the published tip, and server-wallet reads run on blocking workers. A hot-wallet submission copies its key and releases the wallet store before waiting for chain access.

A panic while chain, snapshot or wallet state is held may leave that state partially updated. The next access ends the process with exit status 70 rather than leaving a running node that cannot validate, mine or accept changes; restart revalidates history from disk. A panic in a long-lived service loop (a listener, configured-peer synchronization or discovery) also exits with status 70, since nothing inside the process would restart that service. Network accounting, peer slots, peer health, the discovery address book, mining statistics and the relay rejection cache hold only counters, diagnostics and caches: a panic while one of them was held leaves at most a stale entry, so they stay usable and never refuse later admission, discovery or mining. A panic in one connection's session or one RPC request ends only that connection or request. The [Linux service](../deploy/linux/hashstash.service) restarts failed exits without a start limit.

Read work that depends on operands or state size is charged once it is known and before it runs. A request that reselects or scans the pending pool, namely a hot-wallet submission, `submittransaction`, `broadcasttransaction`, `preparetransaction` or `template`, pays one work unit per published pending entry (selection and backlog) plus its newcomer, at least 256, as each mined block does, rather than the price of a full 4,096-entry selection. A full pool still pays for all of it; a 64-transaction load wave pays 256 units per submission, so the 131,072-unit refill admits about 512 such submissions per second. On a curved Stash (reserve below curve mass), each exact curve evaluation costs 512 work units: account valuation one, `stash_quote` three plus its trade, and a single-order trade estimate two when the order cannot move the price by half the per-block limit, otherwise up to one per input bit for the executable-budget search. A curved `quote` deposit is also limited to the world's total account cash. Governance projections, including every `explorer_governance` page during a decision, pay one unit per inline grant payment. Proposal and mempool pages pay one unit per row and per grant payment, plus one per 32 data bytes for full JSON mempool rows or one per 512 bytes for hexadecimal rows. A snapshot keeps at most 256 payments of any grant, so it pays at most 256 units per grant row however large the grant is. The state root, the snapshot's bounded governance summary and a by-round proposal index are built at most once per published tip. A complete governance projection is built per paid call and never retained. Public-web replies are also charged to the byte budget, like peer replies. An exhausted budget returns a short, retryable error (429 with `Retry-After`) instead of the result and never starts a cooldown, because the node chose the reply's size. The HTTP response limit is the greater of 193 MiB and the hexadecimal envelope of the largest valid transaction at the greatest validated capacity, so a confirmed record stays servable after governance lowers capacity. Public-web errors from local storage, such as an unopenable explorer index, are reported generically and logged locally.

## Parsing and validation

Frame lengths are checked before reading their payloads. Binary peer messages receive a structural preflight before deserialization: counts, record lengths, transaction-data lengths, amount encodings and compact integers must fit their field and network limits. An amount's byte length is bounded by its containing record and network allowance; monetary and share ranges are enforced by validation after decoding. Canonical record decoding then rejects malformed values and trailing bytes. Parsing and expensive mining/peer work run on bounded worker pools.

Governed capacity bounds canonical block bytes and transaction execution weight. A complete signed transaction must fit the block’s remaining byte and weight capacity. Peer record admission is the greatest validated governed capacity plus 1 KiB of record framing. That capacity is the greatest in the active chain's history, so history can be relayed after a reduction, together with that of losing branches that remain competitive: within six next-block works of the tip, with a leaf block carrying at least a quarter of that work. A branch about to overtake can therefore deliver its larger blocks, while a cheap or abandoned branch cannot raise the allowance. The limit cuts both ways: while a branch with a newly increased capacity is more than six next-block works behind, its first block above the current allowance cannot be received, and header presync (below) cannot pass that block either, because synchronization carries whole blocks. Such a node stays on its lighter branch: raising the allowance for branches further behind would let cheap private branches inflate it again, and following the heavier branch would need a headers-only transport message, which this protocol does not have. The value is recomputed after every accepted block, falls again when such a branch is left behind, and is rebuilt the same way on restart. At the 16 KiB preset capacity a whole peer frame is at most about 3 MiB. The four-byte peer length field still permits at most `u32::MAX` payload bytes, including message framing. A valid record beyond that representation cannot be sent by this peer format; this is a transport error, not a consensus-invalid block.

Incoming batches allow at most 32 blocks, 256 loose transactions and 128 locator hashes. One synchronization frame carries at most 2 MiB of block records, or a single block when one block of the validated capacity is larger. A connection's first message must be a locator request or an address request: its read is bounded by the locator overhead plus the loose-transaction allowance, and a first message carrying blocks is rejected with a cooldown. A locator is matched through the header index; answering it never decodes a matched block body or reconstructs historical state. A request lists its download cursors first and its sender's complete active chain locator last. The recipient's allowance covers the genesis capacity and the branch capacity of every block on its own active chain, so the reply uses the indexed branch capacity of the highest hash in the run of shared active hashes that ends the locator, and at least the next block's body, which fits the common ancestor's capacity. After a governed increase that the recipient has validated, catch-up batches therefore stay full; outgoing batches stop only before a block requiring a capacity the recipient has not yet validated, so it can validate that increase first. Loose transactions can accompany catch-up blocks if they fit that allowance and the frame's remaining capacity. Larger transactions are postponed while the relay cursor advances so smaller records can still be sent. The normal 512 KiB transaction budget expands to admit a single larger transaction within the peer allowance. Every frame must still fit the wire representation and local byte/work budgets. A received block must meet its own compact target within the genesis target limit before it is copied or its full record is hashed under the node lock. Blocks already stored on the active chain are skipped and never become download cursors individually. A stored side-branch block needs no proof of work to resend, so it only refreshes the cursor of the source that sent it (its network group, or its address on a local network); that lets a download resume after a restart. Each source advances only its own cursors, and at the 64-cursor limit a source displaces another source's oldest cursor only while that source holds more cursors than it does. A reply made entirely of known active-chain blocks, each linked to the one before, still shows where the sender's chain continues: its last block becomes the sender's cursor, replacing that source's cursor at the reply's start, or takes a free cursor slot, or else replaces the oldest active-chain cursor of that source or of a source holding more cursors, but never a competing-branch cursor. Without it, a fork deeper than one batch beyond the requester's sparse locator entry would return the same shared blocks forever. Resending stored blocks, side-branch or active, therefore cannot displace another source's competing-branch download.

RPC and Stratum use JSON. Their preflight bounds nesting depth, encoded string length, container size and total value count before deserialization creates a tree. The maximum nesting depth is 64. General strings are limited to 1,024 decoded bytes and 6,144 encoded bytes, arrays to 100,000 entries, and objects to 128 members. Designated raw-transaction and transaction-data hex fields are bounded by the actual request body; completed transactions must still fit current governed capacity. Other fields retain the general bounds. RPC/mining trees have a two-million-value budget. Transaction RPC request bodies are checked before allocation against twice the current governed block capacity plus 128 KiB of JSON framing, with checked allocation arithmetic. HTTP responses retain the response limit described above, at least 193 MiB. Collection pages normally target 32 MiB; a single larger record can be returned by itself subject to the remaining response and JSON-value limits. Wallet management requests, HTTP headers and mining messages retain smaller limits. Peer response serialization also runs on bounded workers.

HTTP parsing is strict: the request line has exactly three tokens separated by single spaces, `Content-Length` contains only decimal digits, and chunked bodies and duplicate headers are rejected. A POST is routed, and a wallet request authorized, before any body byte is read; an unknown path returns 404. Bodies are read as bytes arrive, so a claimed length allocates nothing in advance. After an early error the node ends its write side and discards up to 256 KiB of remaining input for at most one second, so the error response is delivered rather than reset. Each connection serves one request.

The command-line RPC client bounds replies from the endpoint it calls. It counts JSON values before building a tree. Fixed-shape replies such as `status`, `account` and broadcast results are limited to 1 MiB and 65,536 values. Preparation replies are limited in proportion to their request. Pending-nonce discovery uses a loopback endpoint's reported `pending_nonce`, and a remote one's only when it equals the account nonce; otherwise it scans mempool pages for the sender's signed pending transactions, reading at most 386 MiB and taking at most five minutes in total, however the endpoint paginates. The generic `rpc` command keeps only the 193 MiB response frame. The client follows no redirects and no environment proxies. It sends plain HTTP only to loopback IP addresses unless `--insecure-http` is passed.

The generic HTTP `submitblock` endpoint retains the RPC request and value budgets, so it cannot accept every large block that the binary peer interface can relay. A large template returned by RPC is not guaranteed to fit a later HTTP submission. Built-in Stratum submits solved jobs directly and does not have this HTTP limitation.

Transaction selection reuses one prepared state for candidate transactions. It authenticates each unique candidate at most once per selection and applies each attempted operation atomically. The node's own selections skip that check for candidates it already authenticated: every pool entry passed it when it entered the pool, a relayed or submitted transaction passes it before the node lock is taken, and a detached block's transactions passed it when that block executed. The transaction ID hashes the exact signed bytes, signature included, so a pooled entry is never re-verified by a later selection. A selection accepts at most 8,192 input candidates. Only opening funds can be committed, so a candidate whose fee and inputs the sender's opening balance cannot cover is dropped before ranking, whatever fee it declares. The rest wait in per-sender nonce queues; only each sender's next nonce is ranked. A failed operation is retried after later acceptances, but each sender has its own budget of four attempts per candidate it submitted, so failing candidates cannot exhaust the attempts of other senders. Creating an account reprices only payments to that account. Reverse nonce order and state-dependent failures cannot trigger repeated validation of ever-growing block prefixes or rescans of the whole candidate list. If the accepted set cannot be finished as a block, selection keeps the longest finishing prefix it finds, down to an empty block, logs the failure and reports the selection as truncated, so the candidates it left out stay pending rather than being judged invalid.

The pool is the next-block selection, at most 4,096 transactions, plus a backlog of at most 256 previously pooled or recovered transactions that lost only to block capacity, within one block's bytes. The backlog bound does not depend on the selection's size, so a selection that reached its count limit still keeps the entries it displaced. A backlog entry competes again in every later selection until it is included, outbid at its nonce, or expires; when a selection that is neither count-full nor truncated leaves byte and weight room that an entry would fit, the entry was refused for validity and is dropped with its sender's later nonces. The exception is a sender whose own attempt budget ran out before one of its candidates was tried: its entries stay undecided. A candidate that failed every attempt it was given counts as refused, so one persistently failing entry cannot keep itself, or anyone else's refused entries, in the backlog. A new transaction that does not win a place in the next block is refused rather than queued, with one exception: a transaction that rebids or extends its own sender's backlog chain cannot be selected before that chain, so it may wait behind it within the same backlog bounds and fee ranking. There is no separate count gate: a better fee rate displaces the weakest entry. Relayed transactions are authenticated before the node lock is taken. Selection runs only when an unseen entry also passes size, anchor, lifetime, funded-sender and unused-nonce checks, its fee meets the least minimum it could need in the next block, the next block's opening balance covers its own inputs and fee, and every earlier nonce of its sender is already pending or arrives with it. The published pool and cached template change only when the selection does. Block execution memoizes each world's clearing that spent curve work by the configuration, opening curve, ordered orders and price bound, keeping the 16 most recently used within 16,384 fills; trade quotes do not enter it. A reselection that leaves the included trades and opening curve unchanged, such as one for a payment or a future-nonce trade, repeats no curve evaluation. A newcomer that changes the included trades clears again under the node lock, at most 1,024 evaluations per world, and that work is not part of its peer work charge. Up to 4,096 refused relay identities are remembered: forged or malformed records permanently, records that fail a check against chain state until the tip changes, and records that lost a selection until the tip or the pool changes. A record after a missing earlier nonce is skipped without a selection but not remembered, so its predecessor can still bring it in on the same tip. Preparation reads the published pool and does not reselect it.

Reorg recovery walks header metadata to the fork point and loads each detached body once, oldest first so nonce predecessors precede successors. Anchor, nonce and funded-sender checks drop stale entries before the selection, which checks no signatures; at most 16,384 detached blocks and transactions are examined, and one selection rebuilds the pool. A selection never includes a sender without an account at the block opening, so a recovered spend from an account that only a recovered transaction creates is dropped; it can be submitted again once the funding confirms. Detached transactions beyond that bound are not recovered locally; peers can relay them again. The new tip is published once, together with its recovered pool, so readers and preparation never see the new tip without the transactions still pending. An accepted block stays accepted when recovery fails: the earlier entries are kept and revalidated by the next selection.

Transactions directly encode one of six operations and a raw data vector. Decoding rejects unknown tags, malformed and noncanonical encodings; operations cannot nest. Signing commits the exact fee and every data byte within block capacity. The weighted capacity rule additionally counts 1,024 units for a new payment or mining account, for each proposed grant recipient, and for each new proposal or support record. Mandatory grant settlement is exempt from the settlement block’s weight because the proposal prepaid its capacity. These consensus rules complement the local traffic budgets.

A cache holds up to 128 permanently invalid block messages for up to 60 seconds. Its key covers the entire canonical block message: an invalid body cannot poison a later correct body carrying the same header. Unknown parents, future timestamps, local storage errors, deferred history reconstruction and side blocks declined by the admission policy below are not permanently cached as invalid.

A block that would not become the tip is fully validated and stored only if its branch is competitive when it arrives. It must carry at least 1/64 of the work of the active chain's next block, its branch must be able to overtake the tip within 4,096 further blocks of its own work, and it draws on a side-branch budget of 4,096 blocks that only new active-chain heights refill, four per height. The check uses proof of work verified first and runs before any ancestry walk, state reconstruction or storage, so cheap blocks from old forks, whose timestamp error lets ASERT reach the easy target limit, cost no validation or disk, and floods of siblings stay proportional to chain growth even where every block costs about two hashes. This is local policy, not consensus, and extending the tip is never refused.

A declined block is not discarded outright. Its header, checked against its parent's required target and its own proof of work, is held in memory, and so is every later block of its branch, checked only for linkage, height, timestamp allowance and its own proof of work. The node keeps downloading that branch from the held header, as headers-first presync over ordinary block replies. Once the held headers claim more work than the tip, the branch is admitted: its download cursor falls back to the stored block where the held chain begins, peers resend the bodies, and those blocks are validated without the admission policy and become the tip if they really carry that work. A block that fails validation then drops its held descendants. A heavier branch whose first blocks were declined, such as an honest chain met after a long partition or after an initial download from a dishonest peer, is therefore still followed. At most 32,768 headers are held; when full, the held leaf whose chain contributes the least claimed work is evicted, so displacing a presync costs more proof of work than it carries. A branch that needs more held headers than that before it overtakes is not followed automatically. Held headers are lost on restart and are fetched again.

Admitted losing-branch blocks are retained and replayed on every start. Startup then prunes the stored losing-branch blocks that have fallen out of competition, those that a child at their required target would no longer be admitted to extend, unless a retained descendant needs them; peers can deliver a pruned branch again through presync. The active chain is never pruned. Use a calibrated starting target for public networks: `init --hashrate` also limits the easiest target to 256 times the calibrated one.

Historical parent-state reconstruction replays from the nearest retained state: the tip, one of 128 recent states (least recently used are evicted first), or an active-chain checkpoint. Checkpoints keep every state within 32 blocks of the tip and two per doubling of depth beyond it, so an ancestor costs about its depth below the tip to rebuild, never the chain height, and side-branch traffic or other reconstructions cannot evict them. A reorganization promotes the new active chain's recent states into every retained height they cover; after one deeper than the recent cache, a retained height whose state was already evicted is rebuilt from the deepest checkpoint below it, at a cost of up to the reorganization depth, and is kept from then on. The ancestor walk on a side branch is charged against the replay budget too, one step per 16 entries. Deferred reconstruction keeps its progress outside the recent cache and resumes on retry; a change of the active chain beneath it, including a reorganization between the retained progress and the target, restarts the work. It does not impose a maximum reorganization depth. Accepted history is still retained and independently checked. Peer locator requests never trigger reconstruction.

Accepted header/branch metadata uses a 256-entry memory cache backed by a derived SQLite index. Each entry carries a skip link, so anchor ancestry on a side branch is resolved with a logarithmic number of index reads rather than a walk over the branch. Canonical heights also live in SQLite rather than an ever-growing memory vector. Deep replay paths use a disk-backed temporary table; canonical reorganization walks disk metadata without constructing a whole branch in memory. SQLite's main page cache is limited to 8 MiB. Every startup rebuilds these derived indexes from fully validated saved block bodies, including losing branches. The rebuild commits in batches of 4,096 blocks, so the write-ahead log stays bounded (a 64 MiB journal limit applies after checkpoints) and is truncated once startup completes; an interrupted rebuild is simply repeated. A stored losing-branch block that fails current validation, and its descendants, move to a `rejected_blocks` table with a log line; a failing block that the persisted tip depends on, or one claiming more work than it, still stops startup. A stale tip hint is replaced by the replayed best tip. Losing-branch blocks that have fallen out of competition are then pruned, as described above. Cache eviction neither deletes blocks nor rejects a valid higher-work branch. This bounds the header-index and traversal components, not the live account state, all allocator overhead, or total process memory. Active-chain history, and with it startup replay time, still grows.

## Binary peer transport

Each payload begins with the eight ASCII bytes `HSP2PBIN`, followed by a one-byte message tag: `0` for synchronization, `1` for acknowledgment, `2` for an address request, or `3` for an address response. An acknowledgment has no further fields. A synchronization payload contains the 32-byte chain ID, then locator hashes, block records and loose transaction records, in that order. Each collection starts with a canonical compact count. Locator hashes are fixed 32-byte values; each block or transaction has a compact byte length followed by its unchanged canonical binary record.

Address exchanges use a separate short authenticated connection after a successful synchronization. Requests contain chain ID, a random per-process 32-byte node identifier, a one-byte presence flag and optionally one endpoint. Responses contain chain ID, node identifier, a one-byte count, then at most 32 fixed 18-byte endpoints (16-byte IP, IPv4 represented as mapped IPv6, then two-byte big-endian port). Zero ports, invalid flags, oversized counts, truncated records and trailing bytes are rejected. The exact count times record size is checked before allocating the address vector. Discovery replies have a separate 650-byte payload ceiling. Both directions verify chain identity; transport identities and discovery identifiers reject self connections. Discovery messages are never accepted in the middle of a synchronization exchange. Peers that do not implement discovery can still use unchanged configured synchronization with a compatible authenticated policy.

Unpinned frames use a four-byte big-endian payload length followed by the payload. Pinned frames use the authenticated 76-byte frame header described below, followed directly by the same binary payload. They do not add a second length prefix. No newline or JSON representation is used on either peer path.

Both peer paths require this binary envelope and a matching chain identity. Transport authentication is separate from economic consensus. RPC and Stratum use JSON interfaces.

## Pinned peer authentication

For a fixed operator-managed network, give each node its own dedicated transport identity and pin the other nodes. Run key generation as the service account in its private data directory:

```sh
hashstash peer-keygen --out /var/lib/hashstash/peer-identity.json
```

The command prints only the public key, creates the file without overwriting anything, and uses a distinct file format that wallet-key loading does not accept. The file is owner-only: mode 0600 on Unix, and on Windows an access list granting only the account that created it (a FAT or exFAT volume cannot hold one, and a warning says so). Never reuse mining, faucet, activity or visitor wallet keys. Peer identity loading rejects symbolic links and reads through one handle that must still be the regular file it inspected: on Unix the handle must have the inspected device and inode, and on Windows the path is opened without following a symbolic link or junction, which is then rejected. On Unix it also rejects group/world-readable files. Protect the key directory as well. Exchange only the printed public keys over an independently trusted channel.

`hashstash peer-keycheck --key-file PATH --miner PAYOUT_PUBLIC_KEY --peer-pin IP:PORT=PUBLIC_KEY` validates a dedicated identity and one or more pins without starting any listener. It prints only the transport public key and rejects reusing the configured payout identity. The Linux installer uses this preflight before installing the key owner-only for its service account.

For every existing `--peer IP:PORT`, add `--peer-pin IP:PORT=PUBLIC_KEY`, plus `--peer-key-file /var/lib/hashstash/peer-identity.json`. All configured peers must be pinned, with no duplicates. In pinned mode, an incoming source IP must belong to a configured peer, and its proved public key must match a pin for that IP. This assumes stable, directly reachable peer addresses; account for the actual source IP when using NAT. Pinned mode never retries without authentication. Nodes started without identity/pin options use unauthenticated transport; authentication requires explicit configuration.

The `HSP2PAUT` handshake uses fresh 32-byte OS-random challenges, both pinned BIP340 keys, the chain ID, format marker and explicit client/server roles. Both peers prove possession under a peer-only signature domain before exchanging block data. Every subsequent payload, including the final acknowledgment, carries a session-bound signature over its direction, sequence, length and payload digest. Its frame header contains a four-byte big-endian payload length, an eight-byte big-endian sequence number and a 64-byte signature, in that order. Changed, reflected, reordered and replayed frames fail authentication. Signature/hash work on large frames remains in bounded workers; frame and traffic budgets still apply. Connections, outgoing handshakes and discovery-only reads have a 15-second timeout. Other peer reads allow 15 seconds plus one second per whole MiB of the highest validated block capacity, with that capacity clamped to the u32 wire maximum for this calculation. Writes allow 15 seconds plus one second per whole MiB of the frame. Each outbound synchronization attempt, including its wait for an outbound slot, has an overall limit of five times that read allowance, and each inbound session twice it. A signed session never reverts to unsigned frames: if its channel is lost, every later read or write fails. This transport provides authentication and integrity, **not confidentiality**.

The private read-only `peerstatus` RPC reports configured-peer reachability, last successful exchange, consecutive failures, transport mode, listener accept errors, and seconds without a new local chain tip. A peer becomes stalled after 90 seconds without a successful exchange; the chain indicator uses twelve governed block intervals, and at least 180 seconds, without a new tip. No-miner periods can trigger the chain indicator normally. Neither indicator proves an attack. Discovery mode additionally reports its enabled state, advertised endpoint, seeds, known/verified counts, bounded peer entries, and persistence errors. Independent configured-peer retry loops remain active in closed mode; public discovery uses a bounded scheduler with jitter/backoff and reserved outgoing capacity.

`hashstash run --no-stratum` starts a validating/relaying node without binding its solo-mining listener. RPC and peer synchronization continue. It does not start or stop external mining hardware, and it is not a replacement for access restrictions on development RPC methods.

## Public authenticated discovery

`--peer-discovery` explicitly enables public participation using the same signed handshake and frames. New nodes prove possession of a valid dedicated transport key; previously configured endpoint pins remain mandatory. Incoming traffic from a pinned IP must prove a pinned key for that IP, and an outgoing connection to another port on a pinned IP must also prove one of that IP's pinned keys. An unrecognized key at another IP may join, but it never bypasses block validation. Closed pinned mode continues to reject discovery and unconfigured peers. Neither mode falls back to unsigned traffic. See [joining a network](RUNNING.md#joining-a-public-network) for seeds and inbound port setup.

Bootstrap accepts at most 16 numeric/DNS seeds, at most eight DNS results per seed, five-second resolution deadlines, and ten-minute refresh intervals. DNS replies cannot nominate private addresses in public mode. A numeric seed or configured peer is an explicit operator choice; it is still subject to handshake and chain validation. Discovery gossip accepts only conservative global-unicast addresses, rejecting private, loopback, link-local, unspecified, multicast, documentation, benchmarking and transition ranges. IPv4-mapped addresses normalize to IPv4 before filtering. An inbound advertisement must match the connection's observed source IP. Advertised addresses are not forwarded until a successful chain synchronization, except the operator's own explicitly advertised endpoint.

The book has at most 256 entries, with at most 32 from one source network, 16 learned destinations in one network (IPv4 /16, IPv6 /32) and two learned endpoints per IP address. A full book replaces entries instead of refusing new ones: configured peers and numeric seeds displace any learned entry, so persisted entries can never crowd them out, while a learned address displaces only an unverified entry, failed ones first and otherwise in an unpredictable per-process order. A learned address that never answered is dropped after three consecutive failures. Duplicate announcements do not refresh lifetime or clear failure backoff. Entries expire after seven days without successful contact; only verified entries persist. A single unknown probe is permitted while a recent healthy peer exists, reserving capacity for established synchronization. Healthy peers cannot be queued behind a flooded unverified book. Entries with equal attempt times, including never-tried entries, are tried in the unpredictable per-process order rather than numeric address order. Prefix diversity prevents one network group occupying both public outbound slots; private-network fixtures explicitly relax this scheduling rule. Discovery runs at most once per successful endpoint per minute, separately from ordinary synchronization. Address responses never include peers configured for this run, whatever source first recorded them, nor peers saved as configured. Every requester receives the same random sample of recent peers for six hours, whatever peers are verified meanwhile, so repeated requests, or getting more of one's own addresses verified, cannot enumerate them; only a sample that already lists every eligible peer follows changes sooner. The node's own advertised endpoint is always included.

The chain-scoped sidecar is limited to 128 KiB before parsing and 256 entries before admission; saved chain/version, endpoint filters, source quotas and timestamps are rechecked on load. A wrong-chain, malformed, oversized or symbolic-link sidecar is renamed to `NAME.invalid` without following a link; startup continues with configured peers and seeds and reports the reason in `peerstatus.discovery.load_error`. Saved verified rows dated after the current clock, as after booting without a real-time clock, remain on disk and load once the clock reaches them. Rows that look expired at startup also remain on disk until this run verifies a peer, so a clock running fast cannot erase them either. Saved future retry times are clamped to one minute. The book is rewritten only when its persisted form changes, and the write and its fsync run without holding the discovery lock. Temporary files use unpredictable names and exclusive creation, and leftovers from an interrupted save are removed at startup; books are never written through a symbolic link. Replacement is an atomic rename on Unix and Windows. Persistence errors appear in `peerstatus.discovery.last_error`.

## Remaining limits

These controls reduce resource abuse; they are not distributed denial-of-service protection. Many source addresses can evade per-IP limits: IPv6 accounting groups a /64 but does not aggregate larger prefixes, so a holder of a /48 or of many networks can still multiply rate budgets. Inbound sessions are not evicted; sources in enough distinct network groups can still occupy the general inbound slots for their session deadlines, leaving only the reserved slots to configured and recently verified peers. At the preset capacity every unconfigured session can buffer a full frame at once, but larger governed capacities admit fewer maximal frames: from about 32 MiB frames (16 MiB capacity), one network group sending maximal frames slowly can hold the shared frame memory until its read allowance ends, delaying other unconfigured peers' frames. Configured and pinned peers keep their own share. A compromised pinned peer can still misbehave. Discovery enforces network-prefix/source quotas rather than claiming provider or operator independence. Authentication prevents impersonating an explicitly pinned key and binds other peers to the keys they prove; an unknown discovered key is not a trusted identity. It cannot guarantee reachability, prevent a routing partition, or make compromised peers honest. A three-node deployment on one provider is not eclipse-proof. Transport is unencrypted, and a node's transport key persists across restarts and address changes, so observers can link its sessions. Accepted block bodies and disk indexes still grow: only losing-branch blocks that have fallen out of competition are pruned, at startup, and the active chain is never pruned. Valid live account/proposal state can grow too. A valid large block or deep reorg still consumes work; budgets bound specific stages, not total wall-clock latency or exact process memory.

Authenticated state depth is not bounded independently of key choice. Tree priorities are public functions of keys, and account creators choose their keys, so each paid account creation with a ground key can lengthen one path by a level: roughly a dozen levels per 16 KiB block at the minimum fee. Accounts are never removed, so such a path is permanent. Tree operations are iterative and cannot exhaust a thread stack, but every update on the path, such as a cheap transfer to its deepest account, which needs no secret key, copies and rehashes one node per level: on the order of ten milliseconds per transaction at ten thousand levels. That work recurs in block validation, template building, every pool selection an admission triggers and every restart replay, and it grows with the path. Consensus deliberately neither caps depth nor charges for it, because a ground path can also push other users' keys deep. The open follow-up is a state commitment whose depth does not depend on chosen keys, such as a sparse Merkle trie over a hash of each key, where sharing an L-bit prefix costs about 2^L work and depth is at most 256. That is a consensus redesign and has not been made.

## Validation

The maintained [hostile-network suite](../tests/network_hostile.rs) covers malformed and oversized frames, parser boundaries, per-IP and per-service accounting, slow connections, invalid blocks followed by honest synchronization, unauthorized mining, and coherent wallet reads under load. The [transport suite](../tests/node_transport.rs) covers relay, synchronization, and Stratum. The [reorganization suite](../tests/reorg_stress.rs) checks governance rollback, pending transaction recovery, competing histories, and database restart.

The peer-authentication unit tests cover wrong pins/chains, role and challenge binding, modified payloads, reflection, replay, cross-session substitution, oversized lengths, sequence violations and dedicated key-file handling. The [authenticated transport suite](../tests/peer_transport_authenticated.rs) runs independent localhost nodes, verifies real synchronization and recovery after hostile unauthenticated clients, checks fail-closed configuration, and verifies observer startup without a Stratum listener. Chain cache tests retain hundreds of valid siblings, exercise a deeper-than-cache higher-work fork with bounded replay, and verify reopening after corrupting only the rebuildable indexes. The [state replay suite](../tests/poc_state_replay.rs) checks depth-bounded reconstruction, fork-point retention and resumable deferral under cache churn, and side-branch admission; the [selection suite](../tests/poc_mempool_truncate.rs) checks that unpayable or repeatedly failing candidates cannot crowd out other senders; and the [capacity suite](../tests/side_branch_capacity.rs) checks that only competitive losing branches raise the peer allowance.

See the [validation record](VALIDATION.md) for completed checks. Reproduce the automated checks with `cargo test --all-targets --locked`; use the [testnet guide](TESTNET.md#repeat-isolated-checks) for a separate live network. Tests use finite traffic against isolated local listeners. The seeded parser corpus is regression testing, not continuous coverage-guided fuzzing.
