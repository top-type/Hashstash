Guide

Hashstash combines a currency, a shared reserve and a way to decide grants and network settings through trading. Hashes are used for payments. Stash is bought and sold through the reserve. During a governance decision, separate PASS and FAIL markets determine which outcome takes effect.

Hashes, accounts and payments

Your account has an address and holds your hash and stash balances. Your wallet holds the private key that authorizes transactions from that account.

To send hashes, enter the recipient's address and an amount in Payments. Your wallet signs the payment, a miner includes it in a block, and nodes verify it. The recipient receives the entered amount; you pay the transaction fee separately.

Mining creates new hashes. Each accepted block rewards its miner in proportion to the work expected at that block's difficulty: one exahash of expected work, or 10¹⁸ hashing attempts, creates one hash.

Exchanging hashes for stash

Buy exchanges hashes for stash. Sell exchanges stash for hashes from the shared reserve.

Each block clears its included buy and sell orders together. Enter the hashes to spend or stash to sell. The quote estimates what that order would receive on its own; other included orders can change the result. The price-change limit starts at 10% per block and can be changed by governance. Excess input is returned through a partial or zero fill.

Stash stays in the account that bought it. To make a payment with its value, withdraw hashes and send those hashes.

Revenue and grants

Revenue comes from the portion of transaction fees allocated to the network and from winning proposal bids. Outside a decision it replenishes the reserve until the curve is flat; any excess remains as waiting revenue.

Governance can approve a grant to specified accounts. The grant uses waiting revenue first, then draws any remainder from the reserve; a winning bid never funds its own grant. Drawing from the reserve steepens its curve while preserving the current price. FAIL sets the same amount aside as waiting revenue, so both outcomes start on the same curve.

As revenue replenishes the reserve, the curve becomes flatter. These changes affect how the price responds to trades; they leave the amount of stash in each account unchanged.

Reading your balances

Your hash balance is available for payments, fees and deposits. Your stash balance is the number of stash units you hold. Home combines your hashes with the estimated value of your stash at the current price.

That estimate is stash multiplied by the displayed price. A withdrawal receives the batch execution price for its filled amount, which can differ from this estimate. Unfilled stash stays in your account. Transaction fees are charged separately, including when an order receives no fill.

Governance: choosing a proposal

A proposal requests a grant or a change to a network setting. Anyone can submit one and contribute hashes to support a proposal.

An auction selects the next proposal for a decision. The proposal with the highest total support wins after holding the lead for the required period. Its contributions become revenue. Contributors to other proposals can reclaim their hashes with Refund.

The selected proposal then enters the PASS/FAIL decision. Its auction contributions remain revenue whichever outcome wins.

Deciding between PASS and FAIL

A decision creates two possible versions of the account balances and reserve on the same blockchain. PASS represents approval; FAIL represents rejection. Your holdings start with the same amounts in each.

You can deposit or withdraw stash separately in each outcome. For a grant, PASS sets aside the requested hashes at the start, using waiting revenue and then the reserve. Those hashes are paid to the recipients if PASS wins.

For example, with 7 hashes in PASS and 10 in FAIL, a payment can use up to 7 hashes less its fee, because it must succeed in either outcome.

The decision counts blocks. PASS wins when its stash price leads by more than the required PASS margin for a strict majority of the decision’s blocks. A tie means FAIL.

When the decision ends, the winning outcome becomes the account balances and reserve used for ordinary payments and Stash trades. Approved grants are paid then. A rest period follows; approved settings take effect when the next auction opens.

Units and encoding

Amounts are stored as integers: one hash equals 1,000 monetary atoms and one stash equals 1,000 share atoms. Each monetary atom represents one petahash (10¹⁵ hashing attempts) of expected mining work. The command-line interface (CLI) and node API (RPC) use these raw amounts; the wallet converts them to displayed units.

Each amount uses its shortest unsigned little-endian byte sequence, preceded by its length. Zero has no amount bytes. Other integers, lengths and operation tags use variable-length encoding. Public keys and cryptographic hash digests occupy 32 bytes; signatures occupy 64 bytes.

Arithmetic uses integers with explicit rounding. The encoding takes three bytes for one hash or one stash: one length byte and two amount bytes.

Transactions, signatures and fees

A public key identifies an account; its private key signs transactions using BIP340 on secp256k1. Each transaction includes a nonce, the sender's next sequence number, a reference block and a lifetime in blocks. The default is two blocks: a transaction prepared from block 100 can be included in 101 or 102. If a reorganization removes the reference block, the transaction becomes invalid on that branch.

The signature covers the sender, nonce, reference block, lifetime, fee, operation and attached data. It prevents alteration after signing. The signature also commits to the network's chain ID, which the transaction does not repeat: another network rejects it. Within one network, the reference block ties it to that block's history, so a fork that keeps that block can also accept it.

Weight measures the work charged to a transaction: its encoded size in bytes, plus 1,024 if a payment creates an account. Grant proposals add 1,024 for every recipient. The minimum fee is ceil(weight × rate / 1,024), where ceil rounds up and governance sets the rate. A proposal's fee is separate from its grant amount. Transactions must fit the block's size and weight limits.

The mempool holds pending transactions. Nodes build candidate blocks by prioritizing miner earnings per unit of weight while respecting each account's transaction order. Miners choose which valid transactions to include.

Accounts and state proofs

An account's 32-byte public key identifies its hash balance, stash balance and transaction counter. Its address encodes that public key for use in the wallet.

Accounts are stored in an authenticated tree. Each node hashes its contents and children; the final hash, called the root, commits to all the accounts. An account proof contains the entries and hashes needed to check one account against that root.

The tree's structure is determined by the account keys, so the same accounts produce the same root. Updates share unchanged parts of the tree, which also lets PASS and FAIL share account data until a balance changes.

The whole-state root combines the accounts, reserves, curves, waiting revenue, settings and governance progress. Each node executes a block and checks that its resulting root matches the root recorded in the block.

Mining and issuance

Mining hashes an 80-byte Bitcoin-compatible header twice with SHA256. A result is valid when it is at most the required target T. A smaller target means higher difficulty.

Expected work is floor(2²⁵⁶ / (T + 1)), rounded down to an integer. A block creates one monetary atom per complete 10¹⁵ units of expected work. Any remainder below one petahash is ignored. Nodes follow the valid chain with the greatest sum of this work.

Blocks and validation

A block carries its header, height, miner public key, resulting state root, an extra mining nonce and an ordered list of signed transactions. The header links to the previous block and commits to the block's contents.

For compatibility with SHA256 mining hardware, the header's transaction commitment, called the Merkle root, is the hash of one system coinbase—a Bitcoin-shaped record containing the miner key and a commitment to the complete transaction list and state root. This format requires the full list to verify that commitment; it does not provide short proofs for individual transactions.

A node verifies proof of work, difficulty, timestamps, size limits and transaction signatures. It then executes the transactions and protocol rules to check the resulting state.

The Stash conversion curve

Deposits add hashes to the reserve and create stash; withdrawals remove stash and pay hashes from the reserve. Let R be the reserve, S the stash supply and M the curve mass. The reserve ratio is r = R / M, between zero and one. It starts at one and controls the curve’s shape.

The marginal price is M / S in consistent units. For net new stash x, the curve uses net cash C(x) = R × ((1 + x/S)^(1/r) − 1). The batch execution price is C(x)/x, or M/S when net stash is zero. Included buyers and sellers use that common price, with conservative integer rounding.

A signed trade specifies its input amount, fee, reference block and lifetime. The block price-change bound limits the closing marginal price. If necessary, buys or sells fill proportionally and unused inputs return to their owners. Fees and inputs must be available before the block begins; incoming transfers and trade proceeds from that block cannot fund another order.

An empty reserve

A deposit of d raw hash units into an empty reserve creates d × share_scale raw stash units and sets the reserve and curve mass to d. The network configuration defines share_scale. This creates a flat curve with a 100% reserve ratio and a price of 1 / share_scale in raw units.

An empty reserve has a zero quoted price. Redeeming the entire stash supply therefore requires a price-change setting of at least 100%; a smaller bound leaves some stash unfilled. The next deposit uses the fixed starting price. Waiting revenue remains separate from this calculation.

Governance rules and timing

Each governance cycle has an auction, a decision and a rest period. The highest total bid must hold the lead for the required period to win the auction. Its contributions become revenue; losing contributions can be reclaimed through Refund transactions. An auction that times out proceeds to rest.

The decision starts PASS and FAIL with copies of the existing account holdings, reserve and stash supply. A grant then sets aside its funding in PASS. Trades can change each outcome's holdings and reserve independently.

Each stash trade signs the decision identity and selected outcome. Payments and fees apply equally to both states and require enough hashes in each. Settlement retains the winning state as the ordinary account balances.

A grant funded entirely by waiting revenue leaves the reserve curves unchanged. Equal prices add no PASS lead; a decision without a strict majority of PASS-leading blocks fails. Prices are compared exactly before display rounding. A block in which either outcome has no stash adds no PASS lead.

Settlement retains the winning balances and reserve and pays an approved grant. Approved setting changes activate after the rest period.

Chain history and reorganizations

The node saves blocks in SQLite and reconstructs account balances and governance by replaying them. Recent states are cached in memory. Complete block history is retained, so storage and startup replay time grow with the chain.

A reorganization occurs when another valid branch accumulates more work. The node adopts that branch's balances, issuance and governance and rechecks pending transactions. Equal work leaves the current tip in place.

Transactions and decisions can change after a reorganization. Additional blocks build on a result and increase its accumulated work.

Peer-to-peer networking

Nodes join through seed peers. With discovery enabled, they find more peers and remember reachable ones. The chain identity comes from the genesis configuration and identifies which network a peer belongs to.

Peers exchange known block hashes to find shared history, then send later blocks and pending transactions. Messages have size limits, and each node validates received blocks and transactions locally.

Transport keys authenticate peer connections. Operators can pin known peers' public keys or restrict connections to pinned peers. Signed exchanges bind messages to their session, direction and sequence.

Authentication identifies a peer; it leaves traffic unencrypted. Nodes need independent, reachable peers to receive a complete view of the network.

Wallet, explorer and node services

The browser wallet reads balances from a node, signs transactions locally and submits them for inclusion in a block. Explorer uses indexes of that node's chain history to display blocks, transactions and application data.

The node's RPC interface listens on loopback. Public sites expose selected methods through a reverse proxy. Its Stratum interface gives mining hardware jobs and receives solutions. A node can also validate and relay blocks without serving miners.

Connections, request sizes and validation work have local limits. Separate consensus rules limit the size and weight of valid blocks.

Wallet storage and offline signing

The browser stores your private key locally without encryption. Keep a private backup: clearing site data removes the stored key, and access to your browser or site can expose it. A watch-only wallet displays balances and prepares transactions using an address.

Hashstash Cold signs transactions on a disconnected computer. Export the network file from Explorer → Network → Identity, load it into Cold, then import and review an unsigned transaction. Sign it there and broadcast the result through Payments → Advanced.

Applications

Chat displays transaction text.

Mail attaches an encrypted message to a transaction. The receiving wallet decrypts it locally. The transaction's sender, amounts and action remain public.
