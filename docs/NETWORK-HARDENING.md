# Network resource controls

Hashstash bounds local resources used by untrusted peer, RPC, and mining traffic. These are node policies: exceeding a traffic budget does not make a block invalid.

## Isolation and admission

RPC requires a loopback listener, including on nodes without an enabled wallet. RPC, peer traffic, and Stratum use separate session and worker capacity. Per-IP counters are scoped to each service; a peer cooldown cannot also disable wallet access from that IP. IPv4-mapped IPv6 addresses share the IPv4 counter, a 6to4 source shares one counter with the /48 its IPv4 host controls, and another global-unicast IPv6 source shares one counter with its whole /64, because one host normally controls that prefix. Each such counter is charged together with an aggregate for its wider prefix: an IPv4 /24, a native IPv6 /48, for a 6to4 source the IPv4 /24 its host belongs to (shared with that IPv4 network), and on RPC all of loopback (127.0.0.0/8 and ::1). An aggregate has four times one address's connection, request, byte and work budgets, so the holder of a /48, which tunnel brokers hand out freely, of a /24, or a local process binding many loopback addresses gets four address budgets in all, not one per /64 or address. The factor of four is a deliberate choice: neighbours in one /24 or /48 who are not one operator share it. A charge is admitted by every record it touches or by none, so a refusal spends nothing. One member's violations cool down only that member; once four distinct members of an aggregate were penalized, or refused because they together exhausted it, within a minute, the whole prefix cools down, escalating from 250 milliseconds to 16 seconds with each further strike. Loopback's aggregate never cools down, so local software cannot lock the operator out. An inbound peer connection from the exact address of a configured or pinned peer is charged to that address's own counter only, so neighbours in its /24 or /48 whose violations cool the prefix down, or who hold the prefix's connections, cannot refuse the operator's chosen peers; their own budgets and cooldowns still apply. Other local networks (private, link-local, unique-local and CGNAT), peer and Stratum connections from loopback, and connections this node opens keep per-address records only. Connections this node opens use records separate from inbound traffic from the same address, so inbound abuse or a cooldown caused by another host behind a shared address cannot block this node's own synchronization with it.

Admission checks run before large reads and expensive work. Request, byte, and work budgets refill over time. Accounting is bounded to 1,024 address/service/direction entries, and active entries cannot be evicted; when the table is full, an inactive entry whose cooldown has ended is replaced before one still cooling down. Temporary cooldowns increase from 250 milliseconds up to 16 seconds with each penalty, and the escalation lapses a minute after the last one, however active the address stays. They apply to malformed frames or payloads and explicit resource violations; ordinary stale transactions and competing branches are not grounds for a permanent ban. A peer that closes its connection between two messages sent nothing malformed and starts no cooldown, and a budget refusal while a frame arrives is penalized once, not again as a malformed frame. An HTTP request that admission refuses is answered rather than dropped. An address cooldown or an exhausted connection, request, byte or work budget returns 429 with `Retry-After`: the whole seconds until the cooldown has ended and the refused charge has refilled. Busy sessions, RPC workers or parsers return 503 with `Retry-After: 1`. JSON-RPC requests keep their JSON-RPC error body and ID; the command-line RPC client and the web interface show that message and the `Retry-After` wait rather than only the status, the web interface pauses its periodic refresh, Chat feed, Explorer reloads and Mail scan for that wait (at most a minute), and the faucet resends once after a `Retry-After` of up to five seconds, otherwise reporting the node as busy and waiting as asked before its next refresh (see [the faucet](FAUCET.md)). A request answered 429 or 503 has not run, so a client may resend it after that wait. A `mine` batch refused after its first block instead reports how many blocks it mined, as an ordinary error, and a public reply refused by its byte budget keeps status 200 (see below), because those requests did run. A charge larger than the whole burst can never be admitted and returns 400. An HTTP connection is admitted before it takes listener or session capacity, as peer and Stratum connections are, so an attempt refused because every session is busy still spends its address's request unit and respects its cooldown. A connection refused before its session starts is answered only after a well-formed request line and a complete head, within two seconds and the 16 KiB head limit, with a fixed reply of under 1 KiB; at most 16 KiB of unread body is then discarded. Each address receives at most one such answer per `Retry-After` it was given, and at most 16 are in flight per listener. Further refused attempts in that window, connections without a well-formed head and refusals beyond those 16 are closed unanswered, as before; a client that waits as told is therefore always answered, while a flood earns at most one short reply per address and window. The public website's loopback proxy carries many visitors and is answered every time. A failed authenticated handshake closes without a cooldown, because nothing has been proved and the source address may be shared; connection-rate limits still apply. Exhausting the budget for a reply this node chose to send fails that reply without penalizing the recipient. Configured-peer retries back off with jitter rather than reconnecting at a fixed rate after every failure. Stratum requires authorization within 10 seconds; an expired attempt closes the connection, and a failed password also starts a cooldown for that address. Connections awaiting authorization have their own slots and leave them once authorized, so unauthenticated sockets cannot occupy authorized worker slots. Stratum admission, with its request unit and cooldown check, runs before a waiting slot is chosen, so a refused attempt still spends its address's budget. Waiting is charged to the source address however the connection ends: each attempt reserves a whole 10-second window from a budget of four windows that refills by half a second per second, and the unused part returns at authorization or close. One address therefore waits in at most four slots at once, and closing sockets just before the window ends leaves it about half a slot on average. At most eight connections from addresses that have not recently authorized may wait at once from one group (IPv4 /24, IPv6 /48; a source on a local network is its own group). The 256 addresses that most recently authorized are not charged, are exempt from the group limit and may use 32 reserved slots when the 128 general slots are full, so the operator's miners can reconnect. That set survives restarts: after it changes it is saved, least recent first, to an owner-only `NAME.stratum-trusted.json` beside the chain database at most once every 10 seconds and when the server stops, and it is restored at startup. A damaged, oversized or non-regular file is ignored with a warning and replaced at the next save. Addresses in prefixes given with `--stratum-trusted ADDRESS[/LENGTH]`, which may be repeated, always count as recently authorized, for miners that are new or re-provisioned. Filling the general slots takes at least 16 public groups and keeping them full takes far more addresses; none of this protects against a distributed flood. A non-loopback Stratum listener requires a password. Authorized solo miners can remain connected while waiting to find a block. An address cooldown refuses new Stratum connections and unauthenticated requests but does not end an authorized worker's requests, because another device behind a shared address may have caused it; the address budgets still apply.

Inbound peer sessions use 16 general slots. Two concurrent sessions from one network group fit its cap: an IPv4 /16, an IPv6 /32, or for a 6to4 source the IPv4 /16 it embeds. Only loopback, link-local, unique-local, private and shared (CGNAT) sources keep per-address limits alone; routable special ranges such as Teredo are grouped by /32. The cap orders evictions rather than keeping the group's other members out: an address holding both of its group's sessions gives the older one up at once to a newcomer from another address in the group, and while general slots are free, a newcomer whose address holds none of the group's sessions takes one beyond the cap. Once the general slots are full, a session beyond its group's cap is the first a newcomer from another group takes over, whatever its age, and a newcomer from the group whose address holds none of its sessions takes over one of them past its grace period (below): the newest beyond the cap, or else the one whose source delivered a new block or transaction least recently. When the general slots are full, configured and pinned peer addresses can use four reserved slots that no other source can take, without a group cap. These privileges, like eviction immunity and the frame memory share below, belong to the exact configured or pinned address only, never to its neighbours in an IPv6 /64: a pin's key is checked for its exact address, so in discovery mode a neighbour may join with any key. Discovery peers this node synchronized with during the last hour have four separate reserved slots, at most one per network group, and their group cap still applies, because any remote can arrange to be verified. For the same reason a recently verified newcomer that finds them full takes over the one held longest past its grace period by a source that delivered nothing new recently. When every slot a newcomer may use is full, it takes over the slot of an unprotected general session instead of being refused, and that session closes at once without a cooldown. No session within its group's cap loses its slot within 15 seconds, one ordinary read allowance, of its admission, so an honest session has time to finish its exchange before sources that keep reconnecting can displace it. Configured and pinned peers holding general slots are never evicted. Recently verified discovery peers have slots of their own, so in a general slot they compete like any other session. Eviction spares at most twelve of the others: the sessions of the four sources that most recently delivered a new valid block or transaction (within ten minutes), the oldest session of four network groups ranked by a keyed hash whose key never leaves the node, and the four longest-connected others, and never so many that fewer than four candidates remain. A delivery counts only for the source whose own message made a block the tip or put a new transaction into the pool, also when that transaction entered the pool after waiting in the admission queue, and never for one that resent known data or merely started an admission while something else changed the pool or the tip. Of the candidates, the newest session of the group holding the most general slots goes. However many groups, deliveries or verified addresses the occupants span, a newcomer is refused only while configured and pinned peers and sessions still within their grace period hold every general slot: sources that keep reconnecting can contend for the unprotected slots, but they cannot displace a session before it had time to finish, nor keep newcomers out by holding the general slots until their deadlines. Each inbound session has an absolute deadline of twice the peer read allowance. That deadline also covers waits for local workers and validation, so its expiry closes the session without a cooldown; a peer that misses an individual read allowance is penalized. The handshake and first message share one read allowance. An outbound synchronization, including its wait for an outbound slot, has one overall deadline of five read allowances, and a discovery address exchange, also including that wait, one of a single allowance. A discovery synchronization that within two read allowances neither made one of its blocks the tip nor delivered headers or new blocks of a heavier branch this node holds is cut off at that point (see [Public authenticated discovery](#public-authenticated-discovery)). Accept errors never stop a listener or abort its live sessions: a reset, aborted or refused connection is one client's queued connection and is skipped at once however often it repeats, an interrupted call is retried at once up to 64 consecutive times, other errors back off from 10 milliseconds to one second, and counts and the last error appear in `peerstatus.listeners`. Stratum extranonces are allocated only for mining sessions and skip values held by connected sessions, so no listener depends on a lifetime connection count. Only peer synchronization failures enter the `peers` diagnostic ring; web and mining clients cannot push them out.

The table gives baseline byte bursts. The private node's RPC and peer byte bursts grow with the highest validated block capacity. They cover the greater of the 66 MiB baseline, the peer frame allowance, or twice that capacity plus 128 KiB for a hexadecimal JSON envelope and 16 KiB for HTTP headers, within the host's allocation representation. A published capacity change resets the allowance immediately: an increase refills existing budgets by the difference, and a decrease, when governance lowers capacity or a competitive branch is left behind, lowers the bursts and the frame memory budget again and clamps tokens held above them. One synchronization session charges a peer address for a largest frame each way, its reply and its push, about once a second, so once two such frames exceed the peer baseline the peer byte burst and refill rate both cover two of them. Other refill rates and quotas do not change. Aggregates have four times each burst. Public-web uses a separate guard. Its byte burst is the HTTP response limit described below, at least 193 MiB, so one maximal valid reply always fits once the budget refills. Connection admission also consumes a request unit. Each Stratum session has a further limit of 8 requests per second with a burst of 16. An unauthenticated session that exceeds it is closed without penalizing its address. An authorized worker's lines past it draw on a separate allowance of 8 per second with a burst of 64 and spend no address request units, so one fast worker cannot drain the budget of others behind its address; such a line can still submit a block, while any other request or share it carries is refused with a rate-limit error. A worker that exhausts that allowance too is closed without penalty. Peer byte and work budgets cover replies as well as incoming traffic, preventing small locator requests from generating unlimited large responses. A reply's work is paid while it is built, before it is encoded: a synchronization message ends before the first block or loose transaction its recipient's budget refuses, so an exhausted budget yields a shorter message, never one built and then refused, and never penalizes the recipient. A message costs one work unit per loose transaction and per block plus its transactions; a receiver also charges its current pool size, selection plus backlog, when loose transactions arrive. One reply's block work is limited to 16,384 minus 256 loose transactions, the 4,096-entry selection and the 256-entry backlog, so a complete reply fits one second of peer work refill; the first block is always sent. The peer work burst grows, if needed, to admit one block at the genesis signature limit with a full loose batch and pool. Inbound peer frame memory is reserved as bytes arrive, never for a declared length, so buffered memory always costs the sender those bytes of its byte budget. Across all sessions it is limited to the greater of 64 MiB and seven largest validated frame allowances, less a reserve of one largest frame for each of the two peer decoding workers: a decoded message is built beside its frame's bytes, and is accounted against that reserve until decoding ends and the bytes are freed. Sessions other than configured or pinned peers share all of it except one largest frame, and one network group may hold the greater of one largest frame and an eighth of that share. At large capacities one group therefore holds at most a quarter of the share and four groups can each receive a full frame at once, while configured and pinned peers can always receive one. A frame beyond its budget closes without a cooldown, and so does a frame that holds more than 1 MiB of the shared budget but receives less than 128 KiB in a two-second window (64 KiB/s) after its first window, so a stalled sender releases that memory long before its read allowance ends; configured and pinned peers are exempt. A message's reservation lasts only while it is received, decoded and processed: it is released before the reply is written and before the next frame is awaited, so a sender that stops reading the reply, or never sends its next frame, holds none of that memory meanwhile. Work units are operation-cost estimates, not hashes or wall-clock CPU measurements. There are two parser workers per service, four RPC read workers, two RPC mutation workers, one server-wallet worker that wallet sends and management use besides a free mutation worker, one worker for the development `mine` RPC, two peer workers, two mining workers, and two validators for solved Stratum blocks. `mine` runs only on a development network, and on an easy-limit one only while no untrusted peer can reach the node (no non-loopback P2P listener or discovery), unless the node was started with `--dev-rpc`, and each block it mines pays one work unit per 4,096 requested attempts on top of its pool work. Worker permits remain held until the work finishes even if a connection closes.

The public-web listener has its own pool of 32 sessions and its own parser, read and mutation workers. A reverse proxy on loopback carries every visitor, so its record has the RPC byte and work budgets above and a quarter more requests (640 per second, burst 1,280), may hold all 32 sessions and is never cooled down. When that proxy sends `X-Real-IP`, the node also accounts the named visitor in a separate record, which the proxy must make trustworthy by overwriting the header; it is ignored from any other address. Each named visitor, and each client connecting directly from another address, may hold 6 sessions and a share of the request, byte and work refill rates: one over the number of visitor shares active, at least 8 and at most 32. Each address that holds a session or was admitted in the last 10 seconds is one share, but the addresses of one /24 or /48 count at most the four shares their aggregate may spend, and an attempt that admission refused does not count. Visitors' refill rates together therefore stay within the RPC rates above while at most 32 shares are active, and the many addresses of one prefix cannot shrink everyone else's share further. The share is counted again at most every 100 milliseconds, not on every charge. While few are active the share is an eighth: 64 requests, 4 MiB and 16,384 work units per second. A visitor's bursts stay an eighth whatever the share, and still admit one largest reply's bytes and the work it pays, and one largest request's work: 128 requests, and work units of the greatest of 131,072, one per KiB of the HTTP response limit (197,632 at its 193 MiB minimum) and the work of a broadcast of a largest transaction at the current capacity. How many others are active therefore changes how long a call waits, never whether it can fit. Every RPC work burst, the private listener's included, grows to admit that broadcast, whose hexadecimal transaction fills the HTTP body limit against a full pool (about 2 million units at 64 MiB blocks), so a valid transaction is never refused, with a penalty, as larger than a whole burst; refill rates do not change. A browser loading the wallet makes about 20 requests, each a proxied connection and a request, so a burst covers about three page loads. Visitors' records have aggregates per /24 or /48 too, as above. A named visitor pays from its own record, then its aggregate, then the shared one, and a charge is admitted by all of them or none, so it cannot spend more than its share of what all visitors share. A quarter of the shared request, work and byte bursts is kept for wallet-critical calls: `status`, `snapshot`, `account`, `fee_quote`, `transfer_quote`, `stash_quote`, `support`, `preparetransaction` and `broadcasttransaction`. Page and script requests and other calls, such as explorer and governance reads, are refused once only that reserve remains, so visitors flooding them cannot deny balance polls, quotes, preparation or broadcast. Wallet-critical calls themselves may spend the reserve; they are bounded by each visitor's share, whose refill rates together stay within the shared ones while at most 32 shares are active. Every proxied connection pays the shared record a request unit when it is admitted, before its visitor or call is known, so the shared request refill is a quarter above what visitors' shares can spend, and a connection whose named visitor is refused, by its cooldown, session limit or rate, gives that unit back. The shared byte and work bursts admit one largest reply, and the work it pays, above that reserve. A JSON-RPC request's unit and bytes are charged before the method is known and may use the reserve. When all 32 sessions are in use, a newcomer from a network (an IPv4 /24 or IPv6 /48, or one address on a local network) holding at least two fewer sessions than the network holding the most closes that network's oldest session and takes its place; the proxy's sessions are never closed this way, and its newcomers count as a network holding none. A few networks therefore cannot keep everyone else out by holding sessions open: shutting newcomers out takes about as many networks as there are sessions. The request line and headers must arrive within 5 seconds, and a reply must be written within 5 seconds plus one per 64 KiB, at most 15, so a client that stops reading a page or script holds its session for seconds. Pages and scripts pay their bytes like other replies. A reply larger than 64 KiB is held against an allowance of 64 MiB of reply bodies until it is written and is replaced by a short JSON-RPC error past it, so slow readers cannot make the node hold a large reply per session; a reply larger than the whole allowance takes all of it, so one largest valid reply is always served. An explorer reply's per-KiB work (see below) is reserved before the page is built, while a read worker is held: that of 50 records eight times the size of a largest transaction at the greatest validated capacity, at most the 32 MiB page budget (6,400 units at 16 KiB). What the page did not use returns once its size is known, so a client cannot skip that work by leaving too little byte budget for the reply; a refused reservation returns the method price and is answered 429. HTTP framing errors and expired deadlines cool down a remote client or named visitor, but never local software on loopback; refused origins and unknown paths, which ordinary browsers cause, are not penalized.

The configured Stratum reporting difficulty defaults to 65,536. Choose it for the expected hash rate and traffic budget. A connection's valid reporting shares at its current difficulty are measured in samples of at least 16 share intervals. A sample that has spanned 30 seconds and averages more than two shares per second multiplies its difficulty for new jobs by the largest power of two that keeps the observed rate at or above one per second; a stream faster than eight per second, the session rate, is raised as soon as its 16 intervals have arrived, measured over at least 250 milliseconds. A stream far above the session rate is therefore raised in one step, and the shares it sends past that rate before it switches jobs are refused rather than disconnected, while noise in a miner's share times does not raise it. Difficulty above the floor or what the miner negotiated halves again while its valid shares average fewer than one per four seconds over at least two minutes. The per-IP request budget still bounds the combined share rate of the workers behind one address outside the operator's trusted prefixes: once other connections there have exhausted it, a worker's shares are refused with a rate error, but its connection stays open and its blocks are still submitted. Each job's reporting difficulty is capped at network difficulty so every block solution is reported. BIP310 minimum-difficulty negotiation must remain compatible with the fixed genesis target limit, including when timestamp ASERT makes mining easier. An extremely easy chain therefore needs an appropriate starting target before ASIC-scale mining. Valid non-block shares are acknowledged without executing the block. Each job caches its coinbase fragments and retains its assigned reporting target. A connection keeps its eight most recent jobs; a parent change discards previous jobs, while a same-parent refresh retires only the oldest. Same-parent template refreshes reach a connection at most once every two seconds. The node rebuilds a same-parent Stratum template no sooner than its previous build's own duration after that build finished; meanwhile connections keep the previous template, which is still a valid block, and refresh once a rebuild is allowed. Further same-parent changes while a connection's refresh is scheduled are folded into that refresh. Waiting for a template holds no mining worker. A template request waits only for a build of the same parent, whose result it then reuses, and at most two builds run at once, each claimed for the tip it extends, so a new tip's template is built at once beside a build for the old tip; that older build stops at its next step (between applied transactions, before the block is finished, and between the finishing attempts of a prefix search) and its request moves on to the new tip. A request still builds at most three times while the tip keeps moving, and is then served an empty template, as below. A solved block validates on its own two permits, never taken by template builds or jobs, so it does not wait for mining workers or builds; it waits for a parser and one of those permits without a deadline. An authorized worker's line is handled before its charges are enforced, so neither a budget or rate limit at its address, nor an address cooldown, nor the block's work charge can discard a solution. Duplicate header tracking spans job IDs on the same parent. Worker records are removed on disconnect, and hashrate samples use a bounded rolling window. Reporting shares never create issuance or alter fork choice. See [mining setup](MINING.md).

The 64 authorized Stratum workers are shared out among sources, since anyone with the mining password can authorize. Workers from outside the `--stratum-trusted` prefixes hold at most 16 from one IPv4 /24 or IPv6 /48 (a local-network address is its own group) and, when any prefix is trusted, at most 48 in all; the remaining 16 then stay free for trusted prefixes, which may use every slot. When the slots a newly authorized worker may use are full, it replaces the competing worker with the oldest valid share (accepted or refused for a rate or budget), or the oldest authorization if it never sent one, provided that is at least five minutes old; the replaced connection closes. A source outside the trusted prefixes only replaces workers from outside them, from its own group when that group is full, so it can never take the trusted share or a worker that keeps reporting shares. A trusted worker, once authorized, no longer counts toward its address's or aggregate's connection limits and pays no address or aggregate budget, only its own session limits and the worker registry; that lets a rental proxy behind one trusted address connect more than 16 rigs. `--stratum-trusted` refuses a prefix shorter than IPv4 /16 or IPv6 /32 outside local networks; `--stratum-trusted-broad` accepts one deliberately. One connection may authorize up to 64 worker names, each with the password, and keeps one worker slot. Extranonces start at a random value on each node.

Wallet status, account, governance, quote and transaction-preparation reads use one immutable published view. They can return the latest completed state while another worker validates incoming history. State-changing requests and historical block reads can still wait for chain access. The view is published after accepted changes, including accepted blocks preceding a later rejected block in the same peer exchange. Tasks on the asynchronous runtime never wait for chain access: peer frame limits and exchange deadlines use the published configuration and the greatest validated capacity, Stratum stale-job checks compare with the published tip, and server-wallet reads run on blocking workers. A hot-wallet submission copies its key and releases the wallet store before waiting for chain access.

A panic while chain, snapshot or wallet state is held may leave that state partially updated. The next access ends the process with exit status 70 rather than leaving a running node that cannot validate, mine or accept changes; restart revalidates history from disk. A panic in a long-lived service loop (a listener, configured-peer synchronization or discovery) also exits with status 70, since nothing inside the process would restart that service. Network accounting, peer slots, peer health, the discovery address book, mining statistics and the relay rejection cache hold only counters, diagnostics and caches: a panic while one of them was held leaves at most a stale entry, so they stay usable and never refuse later admission, discovery or mining. A panic in one connection's session or one RPC request ends only that connection or request. The [Linux service](../deploy/linux/hashstash.service) restarts failed exits without a start limit.

Read work that depends on operands or state size is charged once it is known and before it runs. A request that reselects or scans the pending pool pays one work unit per published pending entry (selection and backlog) plus its newcomer, at least 256, as each mined block does, rather than the price of a full 4,096-entry selection. A hot-wallet submission pays just that: 256 units at the 64-transaction load wave's pool size, so the 131,072-unit refill admits about 512 such submissions per second, and 4,353 units when a full 4,096-entry selection and its 256-entry backlog are pending. The RPC methods `submittransaction`, `broadcasttransaction` and `preparetransaction` also pay their 257-unit method base: at least 513 units, and 4,610 with a full pool. Across addresses, admission selections also share budgets of lock time (see below); a public `broadcasttransaction` that finds the budget reserved for public broadcasts spent is answered 429 with `Retry-After` before its work is charged. On a curved Stash (reserve below curve mass), each exact curve evaluation costs 512 work units: account valuation one, `stash_quote` three plus its trade, and a single-order trade estimate two when the order cannot move the price by half the per-block limit, otherwise up to one per input bit for the executable-budget search. A curved `quote` deposit is also limited to the world's total account cash. Governance projections, including every `explorer_governance` page during a decision, pay one unit per inline grant payment. A request that carries an operation or a signed transaction (`preparetransaction`, `fee_quote`, `transfer_quote`, `submittransaction`, `broadcasttransaction`) pays one unit per grant payment it lists, or per 34 raw bytes, before it is decoded; other methods never read such fields, so a list sent to them costs only its request bytes. Proposal and mempool pages pay one unit per row and per grant payment, plus one per 32 data bytes for full JSON mempool rows or one per 512 bytes for hexadecimal rows. A snapshot keeps at most 256 payments of any grant, so it pays at most 256 units per grant row however large the grant is. The state root and the snapshot's bounded governance summary are built at most once per published tip. The by-round proposal index is derived from the previous tip's: rounds that can no longer change are shared, and only the auction round is ranked again, with the proposals the new block registered, so its cost per tip does not grow with proposal history. An unfiltered `proposals` page seeks its offset by subtree size in the state's key-ordered index, so it reads and pays for only its own rows however many proposals history holds. A complete governance projection is built per paid call and never retained. Public-web replies are also charged to the byte budget, like peer replies, and explorer replies, whose method price is flat, also pay one work unit per KiB they return once they are built. Every public work burst, a visitor's included, admits the work of one largest reply, so such a reply waits for refill rather than never fitting. An exhausted budget replaces the result with a short JSON-RPC error and never starts a cooldown, because the node chose the reply's size. That reply keeps status 200, not 429, because the request has already run: a read can simply be repeated once the budget refills, but a `broadcasttransaction` whose reply was replaced may already have entered the pool and been relayed, so look it up by its transaction ID before resending it. The web wallet treats every broadcast outcome other than a 429 or 503 refusal this way, a lost connection included: it reports the result as unknown, keeps the signed bytes (sending those same bytes again is safe), follows the transaction by its ID and nonce, and signs nothing new from that form until the transaction is pending, its nonce is used, or two later snapshots show neither. The HTTP response limit is the greater of 193 MiB and the hexadecimal envelope of the largest valid transaction at the greatest validated capacity, so a confirmed record stays servable after governance lowers capacity. Public-web errors from local storage, such as an unopenable explorer index, are reported generically and logged locally.

## Parsing and validation

Frame lengths are checked before reading their payloads. Binary peer messages receive a structural preflight before deserialization: counts, record lengths, transaction-data lengths, amount encodings and compact integers must fit their field and network limits. Raw signed transactions from `broadcasttransaction`, the `broadcast` command and faucet claims pass the same transaction preflight, bounded by their own bytes, so a forged grant or data length is refused before the codec reserves memory for it. An amount's byte length is bounded by its containing record and network allowance; monetary and share ranges are enforced by validation after decoding. Canonical record decoding then rejects malformed values and trailing bytes. Parsing and expensive mining/peer work run on bounded worker pools.

Governed capacity bounds canonical block bytes and transaction execution weight. A complete signed transaction must fit the block’s remaining byte and weight capacity. Peer record admission is the greatest validated governed capacity plus 1 KiB of record framing. That capacity is the greatest in the active chain's history, so history can be relayed after a reduction, together with that of losing branches that remain competitive: within six next-block works of the tip, with a leaf block carrying at least a quarter of that work. A branch about to overtake can therefore deliver its larger blocks, while a cheap or abandoned branch cannot raise the allowance. The allowance also covers the stored root of every held header chain that claimed more work than the tip (below): the block where that chain's body downloads resume, whose validated capacity the next body fits. A branch that raised its capacity while more than six next-block works behind is therefore learned from its headers, which carry no bodies, and once they claim more work than the tip its larger blocks are received one validated block after another; a branch whose headers never claimed that much work cannot raise the allowance this way. A version 2 requester declares its allowance in its header request, and the same session's reply holds only blocks that fit it, the first included, so a reply never carries a block the requester cannot receive. The value is recomputed after every accepted block and after held headers are admitted or dropped, falls again when such a branch is left behind, and is rebuilt the same way on restart. At the 16 KiB preset capacity a whole peer frame is at most about 3 MiB. The four-byte peer length field still permits at most `u32::MAX` payload bytes, including message framing. Consensus caps BlockBytes at 64 MiB, so every valid block and transaction record fits that representation.

Incoming batches allow at most 32 blocks, 256 loose transactions and 128 locator hashes. One synchronization frame carries at most 2 MiB of block records, or a single block when one block of the validated capacity is larger. A connection's first message must be a locator request, an address request or a header request: its read is bounded by the locator overhead plus the loose-transaction allowance, and a first message carrying blocks is rejected with a cooldown. After a header reply the next message is read the same way and may only be another header request or the locator request. A locator is matched through the header index; answering it never decodes a matched block body or reconstructs historical state. A request lists its download cursors first and its sender's complete active chain locator last. The recipient's allowance covers the genesis capacity and the branch capacity of every block on its own active chain, so the reply uses the indexed branch capacity of the highest hash in the run of shared active hashes that ends the locator, and at least the next block's body, which fits the common ancestor's capacity, when the best match is that hash. A higher match is one of the recipient's download cursors, possibly on a branch it stored but whose capacity it no longer counts, so a block after it beyond that estimate is not sent. A reply after header requests fits the allowance they declared, and is read within that allowance even if the requester's own has fallen meanwhile. After a governed increase that the recipient has validated, catch-up batches therefore stay full; outgoing batches stop only before a block requiring a capacity the recipient has not yet validated, so it can validate that increase first. Loose transactions can accompany catch-up blocks if they fit that allowance and the frame's remaining capacity. Larger transactions are postponed while the relay cursor advances so smaller records can still be sent. The normal 512 KiB transaction budget expands to admit a single larger transaction within the peer allowance. Every frame must still fit the wire representation and local byte/work budgets. A received block must meet its own compact target within the genesis target limit before it is copied or its full record is hashed under the node lock. Blocks already stored on the active chain are skipped and never become download cursors individually. A stored side-branch block needs no proof of work to resend, so it only refreshes the cursor of the source that sent it (its network group, or its address on a local network); that lets a download resume after a restart. Each source advances only its own cursors, and at the 64-cursor limit a source displaces another source's oldest cursor only while that source holds more cursors than it does. A reply made entirely of known active-chain blocks, each linked to the one before, still shows where the sender's chain continues: its last block becomes the sender's cursor, replacing that source's cursor at the reply's start, or takes a free cursor slot, or else replaces the oldest active-chain cursor of that source or of a source holding more cursors, but never a competing-branch cursor. Without it, a fork deeper than one batch beyond the requester's sparse locator entry would return the same shared blocks forever. Resending stored blocks, side-branch or active, therefore cannot displace another source's competing-branch download.

Transport version 2 opens each outbound synchronization with up to four header requests before its locator request; a session that sends a fifth is refused with a cooldown. A header request carries the requester's highest transport version, its block capacity allowance and a locator: the cursor of its header download from that peer, its eight best admitted held chains, then its active chain locator. The reply lists at most 2,000 consecutive headers of the responder's active chain after the best match, read from the header index without decoding a block body, 250 at a time with the node lock released in between. Each header costs one work unit, as a block without a body, paid before any header is read: when the requester's budget cannot pay for the whole reply, the session closes without building it and without a cooldown. The reply is not shortened instead, because a download holding a counted branch takes a short reply for a chain that now ends short of what it counted. A requester whose locator opens with its tip and that tip's parent, both on the responder's active chain, is catching up on that chain, and headers after its tip are never held, so it receives only the first one. A header download counts the work a peer's branch claims after the stored block where it leaves validated history, without holding any header. The first header after a stored block must meet that block's required target and median time; a later one, whose parent cannot be executed, its own proof of work within the genesis target limit. Every header must also fit the height limit and the future-timestamp allowance, and a header beyond that allowance ends the reply without penalty. Only once the count passes the tip's work is the branch fetched again from that stored block and held, pinned to its download, or held at once when one reply carries all of it. The held chain is admitted when it itself claims more work than the tip; if the peer instead serves a different header at the counted height, answers with no headers, or sends less than a full reply that stops short of that height, its chain no longer holds what it counted: the download ends and its pinned headers are released. An unproven branch therefore costs no storage however long it is. A counted one is held within the bound on pinned and admitted headers below, so a partition is followed once the heavier side's headers are counted if that side needs at most 65,536 headers before it claims more work than the tip, about 11 days of 15-second blocks; a longer one is not. Headers that extend the active tip are not held, because ordinary synchronization fetches their blocks. Counting holds nothing, so it keeps its place however long its peer is away. At most 64 downloads run, and at most four from peers in one network group. Downloads are ranked by the work of the headers they counted, each of which passed its proof-of-work check, not by their number: a header at the network limit counts only the little work it took, so such headers cannot cheaply outrank a branch whose headers carry real work. A new download beyond either bound is ranked against the download that counted the least work, the least recently answered among equals, within that group when the group already runs four: it takes that download's place if it counted at least as much work, and is dropped otherwise, which ends that session's header requests. A reply made only of headers this node already stores takes no work to send and counts none, so downloads made that way only displace each other, never one that counted work. Once the downloads are full, a newcomer must therefore count in its first reply as much work as the download it displaces counted in all of its own. Work spent once can still be served from many addresses, so what bounds displacement is that work, not the number of addresses. A reply that no longer continues what its request listed, because another download or a relayed block extended the admitted header it named meanwhile or its download gave way, is ignored without blame. Header requests then end, as they do when processing a reply fails, and the session's block exchange still runs. A download that holds no new header for ten minutes releases its pinned headers and holds its branch again from the stored block when its peer next answers, as it does when its resume header was dropped meanwhile, for example with a branch whose block failed validation. Released headers fall under the presync bound below. Several downloads of one branch share its held headers, and a header stays pinned until every download holding it has ended.

RPC and Stratum use JSON. Their preflight bounds nesting depth, encoded string length, container size and total value count before deserialization creates a tree. The maximum nesting depth is 64. General strings are limited to 1,024 decoded bytes and 6,144 encoded bytes, arrays to 100,000 entries, and objects to 128 members. Designated raw-transaction and transaction-data hex fields are bounded by the actual request body; completed transactions must still fit current governed capacity. Other fields retain the general bounds. RPC/mining trees have a two-million-value budget. Transaction RPC request bodies are checked before allocation against twice the current governed block capacity plus 128 KiB of JSON framing, with checked allocation arithmetic. HTTP responses retain the response limit described above, at least 193 MiB. Collection pages normally target 32 MiB; a single larger record can be returned by itself subject to the remaining response and JSON-value limits. Wallet management requests, HTTP headers and mining messages retain smaller limits. Peer response serialization also runs on bounded workers, and an RPC reply is serialized, its size checked against the response limit and its JSON tree freed on the RPC worker that produced it, never on the asynchronous runtime, which only writes the bytes.

HTTP parsing is strict: the request line has exactly three tokens separated by single spaces, `Content-Length` contains only decimal digits, and chunked bodies and duplicate headers are rejected. A POST is routed, and a wallet request authorized, before any body byte is read; an unknown path returns 404. Bodies are read as bytes arrive, so a claimed length allocates nothing in advance. After an early error the node ends its write side and discards up to 256 KiB of remaining input for at most one second, so the error response is delivered rather than reset. Each connection serves one request.

The command-line RPC client bounds replies from the endpoint it calls. It counts JSON values before building a tree. Fixed-shape replies such as `status`, `account` and broadcast results are limited to 1 MiB and 65,536 values. Preparation replies are limited in proportion to their request. Pending-nonce discovery uses a loopback endpoint's reported `pending_nonce`, and a remote one's only when it equals the account nonce; otherwise it scans mempool pages for the sender's signed pending transactions, reading at most 386 MiB and taking at most five minutes in total, however the endpoint paginates. The generic `rpc` command keeps only the 193 MiB response frame. The client follows no redirects and no environment proxies. It sends plain HTTP only to loopback IP addresses unless `--insecure-http` is passed.

The generic HTTP `submitblock` endpoint retains the RPC request and value budgets, so it cannot accept every large block that the binary peer interface can relay. A large template returned by RPC is not guaranteed to fit a later HTTP submission. Built-in Stratum submits solved jobs directly and does not have this HTTP limitation.

Transaction selection reuses one prepared state for candidate transactions. It authenticates each unique candidate at most once per selection and applies each attempted operation atomically. The node's own selections skip that check for candidates it already authenticated: every pool entry passed it when it entered the pool, a relayed or submitted transaction passes it before the node lock is taken, and a detached block's transactions passed it when that block executed. The transaction ID hashes the exact signed bytes, signature included, so a pooled entry is never re-verified by a later selection. A selection accepts at most 8,192 input candidates. Only opening funds can be committed, so a candidate whose fee and inputs the sender's opening balance cannot cover is dropped before ranking, whatever fee it declares. The rest wait in per-sender nonce queues; only each sender's next nonce is ranked. A failed operation is retried after later acceptances, but each sender has its own budget of four attempts per candidate it submitted, so failing candidates cannot exhaust the attempts of other senders. Every acceptance retries every failure, so acceptances that cannot help a candidate, such as payments ranked between a Support and the pending proposal it needs, may spend its budget before the one it waits for; the first of a sender's candidates whose budget ran out after it had failed therefore gets one last attempt, which the budget does not pay for, once nothing else is ready (one per sender, so these attempts stay within the number of senders). Creating an account reprices only payments to that account. Reverse nonce order and state-dependent failures cannot trigger repeated validation of ever-growing block prefixes or rescans of the whole candidate list. Finishing the block, which settles its batch trades and pays the miner, is the only step that clears trades, and mempool admission skips it. Every rule that decides inclusion runs while candidates are applied, and a trade's inputs and fee are reserved from block-opening balances, so its fill never decides whether any transaction is valid; admission therefore selects exactly what a finished selection would. If a template's transactions cannot be finished as a block, the template keeps the longest finishing prefix it finds, down to an empty block, and logs the failure with the first transaction it left out; the entries it left out stay in the pool rather than being judged invalid, and later templates for the same tip leave out that first transaction, with its sender's later nonces, until the next tip tries it again.

The pool is the next-block selection, at most 4,096 transactions, plus a backlog of at most 256 previously pooled or recovered transactions that lost only to block capacity, within one block's bytes. The backlog bound does not depend on the selection's size, so a selection that reached its count limit still keeps the entries it displaced. A backlog entry competes again in every later selection until it is included, outbid at its nonce, or expires; when a selection that is not count-full leaves byte and weight room that an entry would fit, the entry was refused for validity and is dropped with its sender's later nonces. The exception is a sender whose own attempt budget ran out before one of its candidates was tried: its entries stay undecided. A candidate that failed every attempt it was given, its last one included, counts as refused, so one persistently failing entry cannot keep itself, or anyone else's refused entries, in the backlog. A new transaction that does not win a place in the next block is refused rather than queued, with one exception: a transaction that rebids or extends its own sender's backlog chain cannot be selected before that chain, so it may wait behind it within the same backlog bounds and fee ranking, at most 128 nonces past the chain's first entry. Because an entry cannot be selected before its sender's earlier nonces, the backlog ranks it no higher than the lowest bid among them and keeps it only after all of them, and the room of an entry left out goes to the next bid: a high bid behind a low one never displaces another sender's entry. Each entry's funds are checked on its own, so past its first entry one sender's chain keeps at most half of the backlog's entries and bytes. There is no separate count gate: a better fee rate displaces the weakest entry. Relayed and submitted transactions are authenticated before the node lock is taken. Selection runs only when an unseen entry also passes size, anchor, lifetime, funded-sender and unused-nonce checks, its fee meets the least minimum it could need in the next block, the next block's opening balance covers its own inputs and fee, and every earlier nonce of its sender is already pending or arrives with it. The least minimum fee allows for what an earlier transaction in that block may create: a payment's recipient account, and for a Support the support record that an earlier Support from the same sender to the same proposal creates; a proposal's own record is always new. Three rules refuse further entries without a selection, until the tip or the pool changes. A transaction at the same sender nonce as a pending one replaces it only if it raises the signed fee per weight unit by at least an eighth and by at least the minimum fee rate, so a sender cannot churn the pool, and the templates built from it, with free equal-rate replacements, and if it also ranks ahead of it by fee (miner fee per weight unit, then signed fee per weight unit): the miner's share is rounded down, so a raised signed fee may earn the miner no more, and the signed fee then decides rather than the transaction ID. A selection that left a newcomer out at its sender's next nonce, with room for it and without leaving that sender undecided, refused it for validity; until the tip or the pool changes, a relayed or publicly broadcast transaction at that sender nonce, such as a copy changed in one data byte, is refused unless it raises that fee rate by the same margins, so execution-refused variants cost one selection, not one each (at most 4,096 such nonces are remembered; the operator's private submissions are exempt). Anyone holding a refused transaction's bytes can resend them once the pool changes, so a transaction refused again after it already set such an entry sets one that holds back only other resent transactions: one its sender signed since still gets its selection. And while the selection holds 4,096 entries (or the signature limit), or its bytes leave no room for the newcomer, a newcomer that does not outrank its weakest entry, by miner fee per weight unit, then signed fee per weight unit, then transaction ID, could enter only by displacing an entry it cannot displace, and is refused; one that rebids or extends its sender's backlog chain is exempt, as above. The pool is published only when it changes, and the cached template is rebuilt only after the selection or the tip changes. Admission never clears batch trades, so a relayed or submitted transaction costs no curve evaluation, however it changes the included trades; its selection's cost is what its work charge covers. Selections for newcomers run one at a time and are charged the time each held the node lock to a budget of lock time, which may fall into debt by at most one burst. Relays and the operator's submissions share a node-wide budget that refills 200 milliseconds per second with a one-second burst; public broadcasts have one of their own, 50 milliseconds per second with a quarter-second burst, which relays never spend. While the node-wide budget is spent, relayed newcomers wait in a queue of at most 1,024 entries and four blocks' bytes, in which each source (as for download cursors) keeps a fair share: it may hold at most one block's bytes, and a newcomer for a full queue displaces the oldest entries of the source holding the most entries (or bytes, when bytes are full), unless its own source holds as many. A public `broadcasttransaction` is answered 429 only while its own budget is spent, so relays cannot turn public and browser-wallet broadcasts away. The budget is checked when the broadcast arrives and again when its turn for the admission lock comes, so broadcasts that waited together cannot all run on one credit; one refused at its turn ran nothing. Private RPC and hot-wallet submissions come from the operator: they wait for a selection in progress rather than being refused, and their time is charged, so relays yield to them. The queue is drained oldest first, at most 256 newcomers and one block's bytes per selection, by drains that every peer message, submission and new tip starts while the node-wide budget has credit, so one lock hold stays about one selection. However many sources relay or broadcast, their admission therefore holds the node lock about a quarter of the time at most, plus one selection (only the operator's own submissions can exceed that), and a waiting block submission goes first: admission does not take the node lock while one waits for it, and a direct submission waits up to a second for it. Clearing runs only when a template is built and when a block is validated or replayed. Templates are built outside the node lock, at most two at once and one per parent: the lock is held only to capture the installed selection and to store the result. A build that a new tip overtakes stops at its next step and is built again for that tip, at most three times per request: while the tip keeps moving, as during catch-up, the request is then served an empty template for the tip then current, which clears no trades and so holds its build slot only briefly; it is not stored and is due again at once, so Stratum connections ask for the full template again once their refresh interval allows. For Stratum, a template that lags a changed selection on the same parent is rebuilt no sooner than its previous build's own duration after that build finished, and is served meanwhile, since it is still a valid block; so selection churn keeps at most half of one core building templates. A new tip is built at once, and local mining and RPC are never throttled. Block execution memoizes each world's clearing that spent curve work by the configuration, opening curve, ordered orders and price bound, keeping the 16 most recently used within 16,384 fills; trade quotes do not enter it. A block whose included trades and opening curve match a recent clearing, such as the node's own block from its template, is validated without repeating a curve evaluation. A received block is checked under the node lock against its stored parent (header, required target, proof of work, Merkle root, side-branch admission and transaction anchors) together with its parent state, captured from the cache or replayed in steps between which the lock is released, 64 replayed blocks in all: for a block that would become the tip, which is never refused, one in its first step and then as many as fit 50 milliseconds at the pace the previous step measured, at most eight, and one for a block that would not, which replays only within the side-branch execution bounds below. It then executes, clearing at most 1,024 evaluations per world, without the node lock, and the lock is taken again only to store it and choose the tip. Execution depends on nothing but that capture, so the outcome is exactly that of validating under the lock; another block stored meanwhile only changes whether this one becomes the tip, and one that no longer would passes the side-branch admission policy against the current tip, as if it had only now arrived. A block delivered again while it executes waits for that execution, and so does its child, rather than repeating it or failing for an unknown parent; the wait ends as soon as the block is stored, before the admission its storing starts, which then yields to the waiting block. Startup replay still runs before the listeners start. Up to 4,096 refused relay and submission identities are remembered, with the reason given: forged or malformed records permanently, records that fail a check against chain state until the tip changes, and records that lost a selection, that the replacement, refused-nonce or full-pool rule refused, or whose sender no pending or queued transfer funds yet (see below) until the tip or the pool changes. A submission of a remembered identity is answered from that memory, as a relayed copy is, without a selection. A record after a missing earlier nonce is skipped without a selection but not remembered, so its predecessor can still bring it in on the same tip. Preparation reads the published pool and does not reselect it.

Reorg recovery walks header metadata to the fork point and loads each detached body once, oldest first so nonce predecessors precede successors. Anchor, nonce and funded-sender checks drop stale entries before the selection, which checks no signatures; at most 16,384 detached blocks and transactions are examined, and one selection rebuilds the pool. A selection never includes a sender without an account at the block opening, and consensus reserves inputs from that opening, so a spend from an account that a pending transfer creates can confirm only in a later block than that transfer. Such a spend, recovered from a detached block or newly relayed or submitted (it is authenticated first), therefore waits in a holding area instead of being refused, provided a transfer in the selection or backlog, or one still queued for admission that the next block's opening could take (its sender has an account and an unused nonce, its anchor holds and the opening balance covers its amount and fee), creates its sender's account (a spend admitted together with its funding is held once that admission's selection is installed, so its order there does not matter): at most 256 spends in all and one block's bytes, 16 per sender with nonces below 16, and per source 64 spends and a quarter of those bytes. Relaying sources together leave local submissions, public broadcasts and reorg recovery a quarter of the spends and bytes of their own. A recovered spend that cannot be held is logged. After each new tip, those whose sender now has an account go through ordinary admission, with its budget; the rest keep waiting while a pending or queued transfer still creates the account and their anchors allow them, for at most six new tips. A spend refused because no transfer creates its account yet is refused only until the pool changes, so it can be submitted again once its funding is pending. Held spends are not relayed and are not in the published pool. Detached transactions beyond that bound are not recovered locally; peers can relay them again. The new tip is published once, together with its recovered pool, so readers and preparation never see the new tip without the transactions still pending. An accepted block stays accepted when recovery fails: the earlier entries are kept and revalidated by the next selection.

Transactions directly encode one of six operations and a raw data vector. Decoding rejects unknown tags, malformed and noncanonical encodings; operations cannot nest. Signing commits the exact fee and every data byte within block capacity. The weighted capacity rule additionally counts 1,024 units for a new payment or mining account, for each proposed grant recipient, and for each new proposal or support record. Mandatory grant settlement is exempt from the settlement block’s weight because the proposal prepaid its capacity. These consensus rules complement the local traffic budgets.

A cache holds up to 128 permanently invalid block messages for up to 60 seconds. Its key covers the entire canonical block message: an invalid body cannot poison a later correct body carrying the same header. Unknown parents, future timestamps, local storage errors, deferred history reconstruction and side blocks declined by the admission policy below are not permanently cached as invalid.

A block that would not become the tip is fully validated and stored only if its branch is competitive when it arrives. It must carry at least 1/64 of the work of the active chain's next block, its branch must be able to overtake the tip within 4,096 further blocks of its own work, and it draws on a side-branch budget of 4,096 blocks that only new active-chain heights refill, four per height, and on a byte budget of 64 MiB (or four block capacities, if more) that each new height refills by four block capacities, so side storage tracks the chain's own growth whatever the governed capacity. Both budgets are stored with each side block, so a restart grants no new burst. On Unix, while the filesystem holding the database has less than 512 MiB free, side blocks are declined and their headers held, so the remaining space serves the active chain. The check uses proof of work verified first and runs before any ancestry walk, state reconstruction or storage, so cheap blocks from old forks, whose timestamp error lets ASERT reach the easy target limit, cost no validation or disk, and floods of siblings stay proportional to chain growth even where every block costs about two hashes. Rebuilding the parent state of an admitted block that would not become the tip, and executing it, are further bounded, because such a block may need only 1/64 of a block's work and still clear trades for seconds, in each historical block it replays as well: one runs at a time, node-wide such work may take 250 milliseconds per second (a four-second burst), and each relaying source (its network group, or its address on a local network) may have at most 256 such blocks stored, regaining one per new active-chain height, and use 50 milliseconds per second of that time. These bounds are checked right after the side-branch admission policy, before the block's anchors, its ancestry walk or any state reconstruction, so a refused block, however often it is resent, costs only its header checks. Its replay then runs one historical block per hold of the node lock, and each hold and the execution are charged as measured; the replay continues only while that time remains, keeping its progress for a later delivery. A side block refused by these bounds is not invalid: its header is held as for any declined block, so its branch is still followed once it claims more work than the tip. One source flooding siblings, or siblings of deep forks whose parent states must be replayed, therefore spends only its own share, and locally submitted blocks wait for the execution slot, without the node lock, instead. This is local policy, not consensus, and extending the tip is never refused.

A declined block is not discarded outright. Its header, checked against its parent's required target and its own proof of work, is held in memory, and so is every later block of its branch, checked only for linkage, height, timestamp allowance and its own proof of work. The node keeps downloading that branch from the held header, as headers-first presync over ordinary block replies; this is how a branch is presynced from a version 1 peer. Once the held headers claim more work than the tip, the branch is admitted: its download cursor falls back to the stored block where the held chain begins, peers resend the bodies, and those blocks are validated without the admission policy and become the tip if they really carry that work. A header download's admitted branch is requested from that stored block in the same way. A block that fails validation then drops its held descendants. A heavier branch whose first blocks were declined, such as an honest chain met after a long partition or after an initial download from a dishonest peer, is therefore still followed. At most 32,768 presync headers are held; when full, the presync leaf whose chain contributes the least claimed work is evicted, so displacing a presync costs more proof of work than it carries. Presync from version 1 peers alone therefore cannot follow a branch that needs more headers than that before it overtakes; header downloads (above) can, up to twice as many. Admitted headers, and those a header download holds after counting its branch, are neither counted against that bound nor evicted. A header after a held parent needs only its own proof of work within the genesis target limit, which on every preset is the initial target, about two hashes, so the work these headers claim does not bound their number. They are bounded by count instead: at most 65,536 together, at about 1 KiB of memory each, and a hold beyond that is refused and ends the header download that asked for it. An admitted chain is extended only from its best header: a header reply stops at a header that does not follow the one before it, and a header that leaves an admitted chain below its best header, whether from a reply, a header download or a pushed block, is held as presync without admission until its own chain claims more work than every admitted chain. It is extended only while it claims at most 16 next-block works more than the tip, since its blocks are needed first, and by at most 2,048 headers since it was admitted or restored. A chain that leaves it below its best header counts those headers on from the admitted header it leaves, whether it was held there before or after that header's admission, and is neither extended nor admitted past them, so a detour through a header held without admission cannot start the count again. An admitted chain is dropped once its best header claims less than the tip by more than six next-block works. Admitted headers are written to the database, the headers one header reply admits in one commit, restored on restart while they still follow a stored block and fit that bound, and the stored blocks they follow are kept by startup pruning. Presync headers and header downloads are lost on restart and are fetched again.

Admitted losing-branch blocks are retained. A full start replays those that the persisted index does not already show to have fallen out of competition (it deletes the others without executing them if the rebuilt index reproduces the persisted tip's entry, children before parents so that an interrupted start never leaves a stored block without its parent, and replays them too otherwise; a stored block whose parent is missing, as a deletion an earlier build interrupted may leave, moves to `rejected_blocks` with its descendants); a start from a saved state checkpoint (see below) replays only those forking above it and keeps the others' checked index entries without executing them. Startup then prunes the stored losing-branch blocks that have fallen out of competition, those that a child at their required target would no longer be admitted to extend, unless a retained descendant needs them; peers can deliver a pruned branch again through presync. The active chain is never pruned. Use a calibrated starting target for public networks: `init --hashrate` also limits the easiest target to 256 times the calibrated one.

Historical parent-state reconstruction replays from the nearest retained state: the tip, one of 128 recent states (least recently used are evicted first), or an active-chain checkpoint. Checkpoints keep every state within 32 blocks of the tip and two per doubling of depth beyond it, so an ancestor costs about its depth below the tip to rebuild, never the chain height, and side-branch traffic or other reconstructions cannot evict them. A reorganization promotes the new active chain's recent states into every retained height they cover; after one deeper than the recent cache, a retained height whose state was already evicted is rebuilt from the deepest checkpoint below it, at a cost of up to the reorganization depth, and is kept from then on. The ancestor walk on a side branch is charged against the replay budget too, one step per 16 entries, and finding earlier progress on the active chain reads at most one index entry per recent state, however far below the meeting point the nearest checkpoint lies. Deferred reconstruction keeps its progress outside the recent cache and resumes on retry. A reconstruction for a block that would become the tip or belongs to an admitted held chain keeps it in one of four records of its own, which side blocks neither take over, even one that resumes it, nor evict; a side block's keeps it in one record that side blocks share, and only progress displaced from that record enters the recent cache, so a side block deferred hold after hold adds nothing there. The post-states of the stored blocks where admitted held chains continue, up to eight, stay outside the recent cache until the chain is adopted or dropped, so flushing that cache never makes a body replay its branch from the fork point. A change of the active chain beneath deferred progress, including a reorganization between the retained progress and the target, restarts the work. It does not impose a maximum reorganization depth. After a start from a saved state checkpoint only genesis is retained below it, and the checkpoint itself stays retained; a losing block whose branch forks below it would need a replay from genesis, so it is declined like an uncompetitive one and its header is held until the branch claims more work than the tip, when it is validated and adopted. Adopting it rebuilds its fork point's state in budgeted steps during which the node stays on its current tip: when the fork point lies more than 1,024 heights above the nearest retained state, from the newest older saved checkpoint at or below it that passes its checks (loading it costs one step and one state's decoding and audit), otherwise from genesis, at a cost of about the fork point's height rather than its depth; a node that replayed all history at its start never pays this. That replay retains checkpoints on its way like a full replay, so once the branch is adopted, reconstructing a state on the new active chain costs about its depth again. Accepted history is still retained and independently checked. Peer locator requests never trigger reconstruction.

Accepted header/branch metadata uses a 256-entry memory cache backed by a derived SQLite index. Each entry carries a skip link, so anchor ancestry on a side branch is resolved with a logarithmic number of index reads rather than a walk over the branch. Canonical heights also live in SQLite rather than an ever-growing memory vector. Deep replay paths use a disk-backed temporary table; canonical reorganization walks disk metadata without constructing a whole branch in memory. SQLite's main page cache is limited to 8 MiB. A full start rebuilds these derived indexes from fully validated saved block bodies, including the losing branches still in competition. The node saves a state checkpoint, the state 31 blocks below the tip, every 1,024 heights once the active chain reaches 1,055 blocks, and at every clean stop once it reaches 32. The state must pass the full state audit first, a periodic save's on its own thread off the node lock; one that fails is not saved and the older checkpoints stay. It keeps the newest two and up to two older ones, about one per doubling of depth in intervals of 1,024 heights. On Unix, a save needs free disk space of twice the largest saved state plus 512 MiB; after a failed save the write-ahead log is truncated and periodic saves wait 1,024 heights, doubling with each further failure up to 16,384, instead of retrying on every block. A start uses the newest one saved by the same executable on the saved active chain: its state root must equal its block's committed root and the full state audit must pass, which also compares a decision's copy of its winning action (omitted from the root) with the committed action; its ancestors' and the losing branches' persisted index entries must agree with their headers and parents; and validating the stored blocks above it again must reach the saved tip. Anything else, including another executable whose rules may differ, means a full start, which first drops every saved checkpoint. The rebuild commits in batches of 4,096 blocks, so the write-ahead log stays bounded (a 64 MiB journal limit applies after checkpoints) and is truncated once startup completes; an interrupted rebuild is simply repeated. A stored losing-branch block that fails current validation, and its descendants, move to a `rejected_blocks` table with a log line; a failing block that the persisted tip depends on, or one claiming more work than it, still stops startup. A stale tip hint is replaced by the replayed best tip. Losing-branch blocks that have fallen out of competition are then pruned, as described above. Cache eviction neither deletes blocks nor rejects a valid higher-work branch. This bounds the header-index and traversal components, not the live account state, all allocator overhead, or total process memory. Active-chain history still grows, and so does a full start; a start from a checkpoint executes at most about 1,055 blocks, though it still reads every stored block's index entry and decodes and audits one full state. A decision's PASS outcome is saved as its account records that differ from the FAIL outcome's and restored sharing the others, so a checkpoint start never holds a second copy of the accounts.

## Binary peer transport

Each payload begins with the eight ASCII bytes `HSP2P043`, followed by a one-byte message tag: `0` for synchronization, `1` for acknowledgment, `2` for an address request, `3` for an address response, `4` for a header request, or `5` for a header reply. An acknowledgment has no further fields. A synchronization payload contains the 32-byte chain ID, then locator hashes, block records and loose transaction records, in that order. Each collection starts with a canonical compact count. Locator hashes are fixed 32-byte values; each block or transaction has a compact byte length followed by its unchanged canonical binary record. A header request contains the chain ID, a one-byte transport version, the requester's block capacity allowance as a compact integer, then a compact count and at most 128 locator hashes; it is at most 4,156 bytes. A header reply contains the chain ID, a one-byte transport version, a compact count and at most 2,000 fixed 80-byte consensus headers, each the parent of the next; heights follow from the first header's parent. The exact count times 80 must equal the remaining bytes before the headers are allocated, and a reply is at most 160,051 bytes. Versions below 2 are invalid in either message.

The marker's last three characters name the peer protocol generation: the release that introduced the block, transaction, state commitment and peer message rules a node enforces (`043` for 0.43.0), not the binary's version. A later release with compatible rules keeps it; one whose records or messages another generation cannot validate changes it, together with the authenticated handshake marker `HSP2A043` and the database's storage marker. Hashstash 0.42.0 and earlier used `HSP2PBIN` and `HSP2PAUT`. Their framing, and for the same genesis file their chain ID, match this release's, but their blocks commit to another state encoding and their signatures do not bind the chain ID, so without the marker the two would exchange addresses and records that fail validation on the other side. A node refuses a first frame or greeting that carries another generation's marker before decoding anything else, closes the connection without a reply and records an error naming the release, such as "peer runs Hashstash 0.42.0 or earlier (peer protocol HSP2PBIN)"; bytes that carry no Hashstash marker are reported as an invalid peer message format. An older node in turn refuses this release's frames as an invalid format and closes, which the requester reports as a peer that closed the connection on a header request, a locator request or the authentication handshake, naming Hashstash 0.42.0 or earlier, another genesis and the peer's connection limits as the possible causes. The `peers` RPC shows these errors. Nothing of the other generation's chain is downloaded, stored or relayed.

Tags `0` to `3` are transport version 1. Version 2 adds the header exchange, and a version 2 node answers a version 1 session exactly as before: header replies are sent only in answer to header requests. A header request carries the highest version its sender speaks, and the reply the version its responder chose, at most that one; this node speaks version 2 and accepts only a version 2 reply. A version 1 peer rejects a header request as an unknown message type and closes the connection, with a cooldown for its sender. When a peer closes the connection after a session's first header request was written in full and before any byte of a reply, the requester reports that the peer may run transport version 1 without header synchronization. A version 2 peer closes a session the same way when it refuses it at its connection limits or evicts it for a newcomer, so one such session is not taken as proof: a peer that closes two consecutive sessions so, without answering a header request between them, is synchronized by the version 1 locator request for ten minutes before a header request is tried again. An answered header request clears the count, and so do ten minutes without such a close.

Address exchanges use a separate short authenticated connection after a successful synchronization. Requests contain chain ID, a random per-process 32-byte node identifier, a one-byte presence flag and optionally one endpoint. Responses contain chain ID, node identifier, a one-byte count, then at most 32 fixed 18-byte endpoints (16-byte IP, IPv4 represented as mapped IPv6, then two-byte big-endian port). Zero ports, invalid flags, oversized counts, truncated records and trailing bytes are rejected. The exact count times record size is checked before allocating the address vector. Discovery replies have a separate 650-byte payload ceiling. Both directions verify chain identity; transport identities and discovery identifiers reject self connections. Discovery messages are never accepted in the middle of a synchronization exchange. Peers that do not implement discovery can still use unchanged configured synchronization with a compatible authenticated policy.

Unpinned frames use a four-byte big-endian payload length followed by the payload. Pinned frames use the authenticated 76-byte frame header described below, followed directly by the same binary payload. They do not add a second length prefix. No newline or JSON representation is used on either peer path.

Both peer paths require this binary envelope and a matching chain identity. Transport authentication is separate from economic consensus. RPC and Stratum use JSON interfaces.

## Pinned peer authentication

For a fixed operator-managed network, give each node its own dedicated transport identity and pin the other nodes. Run key generation as the service account in its private data directory:

```sh
hashstash peer-keygen --out /var/lib/hashstash/peer-identity.json
```

The command prints only the public key, creates the file without overwriting anything, and uses a distinct file format that wallet-key loading does not accept. The file is owner-only: mode 0600 on Unix, and on Windows an access list granting only the account that created it (a FAT or exFAT volume cannot hold one, and a warning says so). Never reuse mining, faucet, activity or visitor wallet keys. Peer identity loading rejects symbolic links and reads through one handle that must still be the regular file it inspected: on Unix the handle must have the inspected device and inode, and on Windows the path is opened without following a symbolic link or junction, which is then rejected. On Unix it also rejects group/world-readable files, and on Windows a file whose access list lets in any account other than its own, SYSTEM and Administrators. Protect the key directory as well. Exchange only the printed public keys over an independently trusted channel.

`hashstash peer-keycheck --key-file PATH --miner PAYOUT_PUBLIC_KEY --peer-pin IP:PORT=PUBLIC_KEY` validates a dedicated identity and one or more pins without starting any listener. It prints only the transport public key and rejects reusing the configured payout identity. The Linux installer uses this preflight before installing the key owner-only for its service account.

For every existing `--peer IP:PORT`, add `--peer-pin IP:PORT=PUBLIC_KEY`, plus `--peer-key-file /var/lib/hashstash/peer-identity.json`. All configured peers must be pinned, with no duplicates. In pinned mode, an incoming source IP must belong to a configured peer, and its proved public key must match a pin for that IP. This assumes stable, directly reachable peer addresses; account for the actual source IP when using NAT. Pinned mode never retries without authentication. Nodes started without identity/pin options use unauthenticated transport; authentication requires explicit configuration.

The `HSP2A043` handshake uses fresh 32-byte OS-random challenges, both pinned BIP340 keys, the chain ID, format marker and explicit client/server roles. Both peers prove possession under a peer-only signature domain before exchanging block data. Every subsequent payload, including the final acknowledgment, carries a session-bound signature over its direction, sequence, length and payload digest. Its frame header contains a four-byte big-endian payload length, an eight-byte big-endian sequence number and a 64-byte signature, in that order. Changed, reflected, reordered and replayed frames fail authentication. Signature/hash work on large frames remains in bounded workers; frame and traffic budgets still apply. Connections, outgoing handshakes and discovery-only reads have a 15-second timeout. Other peer reads allow 15 seconds plus one second per whole MiB of the largest frame at the highest validated block capacity, which carries that capacity's blocks and as much again of loose transactions (about 18 seconds at the 16 KiB preset capacity). Writes allow 15 seconds plus one second per whole MiB of the frame, so a largest frame is read for as long as its sender may take to write it. An inbound connection's handshake and first message, a request that carries no blocks, share the allowance for the largest such request instead (15 seconds at the preset capacity). Each outbound synchronization attempt, including its wait for an outbound slot, has an overall limit of five times that read allowance, each discovery address exchange, including that wait, one allowance, and each inbound session twice it. A signed session never reverts to unsigned frames: if its channel is lost, every later read or write fails. This transport provides authentication and integrity, **not confidentiality**.

The private read-only `peerstatus` RPC reports configured-peer reachability, last successful exchange, consecutive failures, transport mode, the peer transport version (`transport_version`), the peers currently synchronized by blocks only after closing two consecutive sessions on a header request (`version1_peers`), the block capacity allowance for peer records (`capacity_allowance`), listener accept errors, and seconds without a new local chain tip. A peer becomes stalled after 90 seconds without a successful exchange; the chain indicator uses twelve governed block intervals, and at least 180 seconds, without a new tip. No-miner periods can trigger the chain indicator normally. Neither indicator proves an attack. Discovery mode additionally reports its enabled state, advertised endpoint, seeds, known/verified counts, bounded peer entries, and persistence errors. Independent configured-peer retry loops remain active in closed mode; public discovery uses a bounded scheduler with jitter/backoff and reserved outgoing capacity.

`hashstash run --no-stratum` starts a validating/relaying node without binding its solo-mining listener. RPC and peer synchronization continue. It does not start or stop external mining hardware, and it is not a replacement for access restrictions on development RPC methods.

## Public authenticated discovery

`--peer-discovery` explicitly enables public participation using the same signed handshake and frames. New nodes prove possession of a valid dedicated transport key; previously configured endpoint pins remain mandatory. Incoming traffic from a pinned IP must prove a pinned key for that IP, and an outgoing connection to another port on a pinned IP must also prove one of that IP's pinned keys. An unrecognized key at another IP may join, but it never bypasses block validation. Closed pinned mode continues to reject discovery and unconfigured peers. Neither mode falls back to unsigned traffic. See [joining a network](RUNNING.md#joining-a-public-network) for seeds and inbound port setup.

Bootstrap accepts at most 16 numeric/DNS seeds, at most eight DNS results per seed lookup, five-second resolution deadlines, and ten-minute refresh intervals, one minute while no peer has answered for ten minutes, as after a lost connection or before the first answer. DNS replies cannot nominate private addresses in public mode. A numeric seed or configured peer is an explicit operator choice; it is still subject to handshake and chain validation. Discovery gossip accepts only conservative global-unicast addresses, rejecting private, loopback, link-local, unspecified, multicast, documentation, benchmarking and transition ranges. IPv4-mapped addresses normalize to IPv4 before filtering. An inbound advertisement must match the connection's observed source IP. Advertised addresses are not forwarded until a successful chain synchronization, except the operator's own explicitly advertised endpoint.

The book has at most 256 entries, with at most 32 from one source network, 16 learned destinations in one network (IPv4 /16, IPv6 /32) and two learned endpoints per IP address. A learned address that finds its IP address full displaces an unverified entry there that another source supplied, when that source holds more of the address's entries than the newcomer's source; one that finds its network group full displaces one whose source would still hold more of the group's entries than the newcomer's; the most-held source's entries go first, failed ones first among those. An inbound peer announcing its own address displaces any unverified entry there. Sources that each gossiped a bogus port for an honest node's address therefore cannot keep its real endpoint out, entries one source gossiped in cannot squat a network group, and no source can push another's entry out by gossiping more ports. DNS seed results, like configured peers and numeric seeds, are operator input: the learned quotas do not apply to them and they are retried at least once a minute, though DNS still cannot nominate private addresses. One seed holds at most 16 results, two lookups' worth; a result that never answered is dropped after three consecutive failures, and the seed's next lookup adds it again. A full book replaces entries instead of refusing new ones: configured peers and numeric seeds displace any other entry, so persisted entries can never crowd them out, while a DNS seed result or a learned address displaces only an unverified learned entry, never a verified peer or an anchor, failed ones first and otherwise in an unpredictable per-process order. A learned address or seed result that never answered is dropped after three consecutive failures, and expires a day after it was added however rarely it was tried. Duplicate announcements do not refresh lifetime or clear failure backoff. Entries expire after seven days without successful contact; only verified entries persist. A single unknown probe is permitted while a recent healthy peer exists, reserving capacity for established synchronization, and unknown entries in a network group that holds such a peer are probed at most once per ten minutes. Healthy peers cannot be queued behind a flooded unverified book.

A discovery synchronization counts as a success when a block the peer delivered became the tip; when it delivered headers or new blocks of a branch that claims more work than the tip, which this node holds but has not adopted yet, as when it switches to a heavier branch that forked more than one batch deep; or when the exchange, with its address exchange, completed within one read allowance without anything new, which shows that the peer is not ahead. In the first two cases the peer may hold more and is synchronized again at once. A turn that delivered nothing new and took longer is a failure, so a verified endpoint that answers each step just inside its allowance neither keeps its verification fresh nor keeps its turn. A synchronization that has done neither of the first two within two read allowances is cut off: a new transaction, which any funded sender can supply, or side blocks and headers of a branch that claims no more work than the tip, which can be cheap, do not keep it going. Failures back off as configured peers do, up to a minute, and beyond six consecutive ones double up to an hour, but only while other entries answer meanwhile: when none does, the node more likely lost its own connectivity, so every entry is retried at least once a minute and the node reconnects soon after its connectivity returns. Configured peers, numeric seeds and DNS seed results are always retried at least once a minute. Discovery has three outbound slots with roles. One is reserved for the operator's chosen peers: configured peers, numeric seeds, and DNS seed results that answered their last contact. It takes one whose last session advanced the tip or a heavier branch at once, and otherwise one not tried for 15 seconds, least recently tried first; no other entry ever takes it, and it waits while none is due. A seed result that never answered, or failed or stalled since it last did, is an ordinary entry until it answers again, so a seed returning unreachable or slow-walking endpoints cannot keep the reserved slot busy with them. The synchronizing slot takes, in order, a peer whose last session advanced the tip or a heavier branch, at once, since it may hold more; a peer that advanced the tip within the last hour and has not failed since, when not tried for 15 seconds; then the verified peer tried least recently. The exploring slot probes unknown entries first and otherwise joins the rotation. However many verified addresses an attacker holds in the book, and however its endpoints pace their sessions or whatever transactions they deliver, the operator's chosen peers are therefore synchronized about every 15 seconds plus the time their own sessions take. Entries with equal attempt times, including never-tried entries, are tried in the unpredictable per-process order rather than numeric address order. Prefix diversity prevents one network group occupying both general outbound slots, or one of them while the reserved slot holds a peer in that group; the reserved slot itself is exempt, since the operator chose its peers, and private-network fixtures explicitly relax this scheduling rule. The two most recent outbound peers whose blocks became the tip, in distinct groups, are saved with the book as anchors and tried first, once each, after a restart, so verified addresses an attacker adds to the book meanwhile cannot displace the peers that last advanced this node; a peer that merely answered is no anchor. Discovery runs at most once per successful endpoint per minute, separately from ordinary synchronization. Address responses never include peers configured for this run, whatever source first recorded them, nor peers saved as configured. Every requester receives the same random sample of recent peers for six hours, whatever peers are verified meanwhile, so repeated requests, or getting more of one's own addresses verified, cannot enumerate them; only a sample that already lists every eligible peer follows changes sooner. The node's own advertised endpoint is always included.

The chain-scoped sidecar is limited to 128 KiB before parsing and 256 entries before admission; saved chain/version, endpoint filters, source quotas and timestamps are rechecked on load. A wrong-chain, malformed, oversized or symbolic-link sidecar is renamed to `NAME.invalid` without following a link; startup continues with configured peers and seeds and reports the reason in `peerstatus.discovery.load_error`. Saved verified rows dated after the current clock, as after booting without a real-time clock, remain on disk and load once the clock reaches them. Rows that look expired at startup also remain on disk until this run verifies a peer, so a clock running fast cannot erase them either. Saved future retry times are clamped to one minute. The book is rewritten only when its persisted form changes, and the write and its fsync run without holding the discovery lock. Temporary files use unpredictable names and exclusive creation, and leftovers from an interrupted save are removed at startup; books are never written through a symbolic link. Replacement is an atomic rename on Unix and Windows. Persistence errors appear in `peerstatus.discovery.last_error`.

## Remaining limits

These controls reduce resource abuse; they are not distributed denial-of-service protection. Many source addresses can still evade per-address limits: an aggregate bounds one /24 or /48 to four address budgets, but a holder of many such prefixes multiplies them, and an aggregate under attack is shared with the honest neighbours in its prefix. Eviction lets newcomers displace slow sessions, but sources in enough groups can keep displacing unprotected sessions in turn once their grace period has passed, including honest ones that have not delivered anything new recently, or keep every unprotected slot within its grace period so that newcomers are refused meanwhile; once the general slots are full, several addresses inside one network group can likewise contend for its two sessions. At large governed capacities four network groups sending maximal frames that keep arriving at 64 KiB/s can still hold the shared frame memory until their read allowances end, delaying other unconfigured peers' frames; configured and pinned peers keep their own share. Discovery runs three outbound sessions at a time, one of them reserved for the operator's chosen peers, and nodes do not push new blocks, so blocks travel only when a node synchronizes; peers that recently advanced this node, rather than all verified addresses, set its pace, and any source that relays a block first can earn that preference until it fails or stalls. Anchors, prefix diversity and the slots reserved for the operator's chosen peers make an eclipse harder, not impossible. A compromised pinned peer can still misbehave. Discovery enforces network-prefix/source quotas rather than claiming provider or operator independence. Authentication prevents impersonating an explicitly pinned key and binds other peers to the keys they prove; an unknown discovered key is not a trusted identity. It cannot guarantee reachability, prevent a routing partition, or make compromised peers honest. A three-node deployment on one provider is not eclipse-proof. Transport is unencrypted, and a node's transport key persists across restarts and address changes, so observers can link its sessions. Accepted block bodies and disk indexes still grow: only losing-branch blocks that have fallen out of competition are pruned, at startup, and the active chain is never pruned. Held headers of a branch that claimed more work than the tip are kept in memory as well as on disk, several hundred bytes each, until that branch's blocks arrive or it falls behind: following a branch whose fork lies hundreds of thousands of headers deep uses that much memory meanwhile, and a header download reads such a branch's headers twice. Valid live account/proposal state can grow too. A valid large block or deep reorg still consumes work; budgets bound specific stages, not total wall-clock latency or exact process memory.

Authenticated state depth no longer follows key choice. The former treap's priorities were public functions of keys, so each paid account creation with a ground key could lengthen one permanent path by a level, and every update on that path copied and rehashed one node per level. State maps are now committed by a Patricia trie over hashed keys ([state commitments](CONSENSUS.md#state-commitments)): no entry lies under more than 256 branches, and pushing a key L levels below its natural depth of about log2(n) costs about 2^L hash evaluations, so an update copies and rehashes a few dozen nodes in practice, and never more than 257, whatever keys accounts, proposals and supports use. Ordered access uses an uncommitted weight-balanced index whose height is logarithmic for any keys. The [depth suite](../tests/treap_depth.rs) checks both against the former ladder and against grinding the trie itself. What remains is volume: valid state still grows with every account and proposal, and each update costs logarithmically more as it does.

## Validation

The maintained [hostile-network suite](../tests/network_hostile.rs) covers malformed and oversized frames, parser boundaries, per-IP and per-service accounting, slow connections, invalid blocks followed by honest synchronization, unauthorized mining, and coherent wallet reads under load. The [transport suite](../tests/node_transport.rs) covers relay, synchronization, and Stratum. The [reorganization suite](../tests/reorg_stress.rs) checks governance rollback, pending transaction recovery, competing histories, and database restart.

The peer-authentication unit tests cover wrong pins/chains, role and challenge binding, modified payloads, reflection, replay, cross-session substitution, oversized lengths, sequence violations and dedicated key-file handling. The [authenticated transport suite](../tests/peer_transport_authenticated.rs) runs independent localhost nodes, verifies real synchronization and recovery after hostile unauthenticated clients, checks fail-closed configuration, and verifies observer startup without a Stratum listener. Chain cache tests retain hundreds of valid siblings, exercise a deeper-than-cache higher-work fork with bounded replay, and verify reopening after corrupting only the rebuildable indexes. The [state replay suite](../tests/poc_state_replay.rs) checks depth-bounded reconstruction, fork-point retention and resumable deferral under cache churn, and side-branch admission; the [selection suite](../tests/poc_mempool_truncate.rs) checks that unpayable or repeatedly failing candidates cannot crowd out other senders; and the [capacity suite](../tests/side_branch_capacity.rs) checks that only competitive losing branches raise the peer allowance. The [catch-up suite](../tests/peer_catch_up_capacity.rs) checks that a branch that raised its capacity far behind is followed once its headers claim more work than the tip, and not before. The node's header synchronization tests follow a branch longer than the presync bound and restore admitted headers after a restart, and the [transport suite](../tests/node_transport.rs) checks that version 1 sessions are unchanged and a version 1 peer's refusal of header requests is reported and followed by block synchronization.

See the [validation record](VALIDATION.md) for completed checks. Reproduce the automated checks with `cargo test --all-targets --locked`; use the [testnet guide](TESTNET.md#repeat-isolated-checks) for a separate live network. Tests use finite traffic against isolated local listeners. Each parser of hostile input also has a [fuzz target](VALIDATION.md#fuzzing): the Rust suite runs them from fixed seeds and continuous integration runs them under libFuzzer for bounded times, each nightly run continuing from the corpus the previous one kept. A bounded run that finds nothing does not show that no failing input exists.
